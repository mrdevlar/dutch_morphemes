From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`kosten`** is a fascinating example of morphological ambiguity and semantic shift. Depending on the context, it functions as either a **verb** (infinitive) or a **noun** (plural).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

---

### 1. Morphemic Segmentation
Regardless of whether it is used as a verb or a noun, the word consists of two morphemes:

> **`kost`** + **`-en`**

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root/Stem: `kost-`
*   **Type:** Lexical Morpheme (Free Morpheme).
*   **Function:** Carries the core semantic meaning of the word.
*   **Meaning:**
    *   In the context of the **noun**, it refers to "cost," "expense," or "livelihood."
    *   In the context of the **verb**, it refers to the action of having a price or value.
    *   *Note:* In modern Dutch, `kost` can stand alone as a singular noun (e.g., *de kost* meaning "the livelihood" or "food"), making it a **free morpheme**. However, within the word `kosten`, it functions as the **stem**.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Grammatical Morpheme (Bound Morpheme / Inflectional Affix).
*   **Function:** This suffix is polyfunctional in Dutch. Its specific meaning depends on the part of speech of the root.
    1.  **As a Verb:** It is the **Infinitive Marker**. In Dutch, the standard infinitive ending for weak verbs is `-en` (e.g., *lopen*, *werken*, *kosten*).
    2.  **As a Noun:** It is the **Plural Marker**. For many Dutch nouns ending in a voiceless consonant (like `t`), the plural is formed by adding `-en` (e.g., *de kat* → *de katten*, *de kost* → *de kosten*).

### 3. Synchronic vs. Diachronic Analysis

To fully understand the word linguistically, we must look at it in two ways:

#### Synchronic (Current Language Structure)
In modern Dutch, the word is ambiguous without context:
*   **Verb:** *Hoeveel **kosten** deze schoenen?* (How much **do** these shoes **cost**?)
    *   Structure: Stem (`kost`) + Infinitive Suffix (`-en`).
*   **Noun:** *De **kosten** zijn hoog.* (The **costs** are high.)
    *   Structure: Singular Noun (`kost`) + Plural Suffix (`-en`).

#### Diachronic (Historical/Etymological)
The history of the root `kost` reveals a significant **semantic shift**.

*   **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** The root is reconstructed as **`*ǵeh₁-`**, meaning "to choose, gather, or pick."
*   **Proto-Germanic:** Evolved into **`*kōstōną`**, meaning "to try, test, or taste."
*   **Old High German / Middle Dutch:** The word became *kostan* / *costen*.
*   **Semantic Shift:**
    1.  **Original Meaning:** To taste or test something (to see if it is good).
    2.  **Intermediate Meaning:** To try something out (to test its value).
    3.  **Modern Meaning:** To require a price (to determine the value).
    *   *Linguistic Note:* This is similar to the English word "cost," which shares the same lineage. In German, *kosten* still retains both meanings: "to cost" and "to taste." In Dutch, the "taste" meaning has largely been replaced by *proeven*, though *kosten* remains the word for price.

### 4. Phonological Note
*   **Pronunciation:** /ˈkɔs.tə(n)/
*   **Voicing:** The root ends in a voiceless alveolar stop `/t/`. The suffix begins with a vowel. There is no voicing assimilation here (the `t` remains voiceless).
*   **Schwa:** In rapid speech, the final `-n` of the suffix is often dropped or nasalized, resulting in a schwa sound `/ə/` at the end.

Linguistically, **`kosten`** is a **monomorphemic root** (`kost`) combined with a **polyfunctional inflectional suffix** (`-en`). Its complexity lies not in its structure, but in its dual grammatical function (verb vs. noun) and its historical evolution from "tasting" to "pricing."
