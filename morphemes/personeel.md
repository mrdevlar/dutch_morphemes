From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`personeel`** (meaning "staff," "personnel," or "employees") is a fascinating example of a **loanword** that has been integrated into Dutch morphology.

While modern Dutch speakers often treat it as a single, unanalyzable unit (a root), it can be broken down etymologically and morphologically into two distinct parts.

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:
> **`persoon`** + **`-eel`**

*(Note: In the actual spelling, the vowel changes slightly from `oo` to `oe` to accommodate the French/Latin root structure, resulting in `personeel`.)*

---

### 2. Detailed Explanation of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `persoon-` (or `person-`)
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (in Dutch) / Bound root (in the loan context).
*   **Meaning:** "Person" or "individual."
*   **Etymology:** This comes from the Latin word **`persona`**, originally meaning a theatrical mask, then a character, and finally a human being.
*   **Dutch Cognate:** The independent Dutch word is **`persoon`**.
*   **Function:** It provides the semantic core of the word. It defines *who* the word is about (people).

#### B. The Suffix: `-eel`
*   **Type:** Derivational suffix (bound morpheme).
*   **Origin:** French **`-el`** (from Latin **`-alis`**).
*   **Function:** Adjectival. It turns a noun into an adjective meaning "relating to" or "characterized by."
*   **Examples in Dutch:** *nationaal* (national), *regionaal* (regional), *professioneel* (professional).
*   **Meaning in this context:** "Relating to persons."

---

### 3. Grammatical Process: Nominalization
The most interesting linguistic feature of `personeel` is its grammatical category shift.

1.  **Original State (Adjective):** In French (*personnel*) and originally in Dutch, the combination of *person* + *-eel* created an **adjective**.
    *   *Meaning:* "Pertaining to persons."
2.  **Shift (Nominalization):** Through a process called **substantivation** (or zero-derivation), the adjective was reanalyzed as a **noun**.
    *   *Mechanism:* Metonymy. The concept "that which relates to persons" (the department, the list, the group) became the word for the group itself.
    *   *Current State:* It is now a neuter noun (**`het` personeel**).

### 4. Synchronic vs. Diachronic Analysis
To fully understand the word, one must distinguish between how it is analyzed historically versus how it functions today.

*   **Diachronic (Historical) View:** It is clearly a compound of two morphemes: *person* + *-al*.
*   **Synchronic (Modern) View:** To a contemporary Dutch speaker, `personeel` is often treated as a **monomorphemic root**.
    *   Speakers do not actively derive new words from it (e.g., you cannot say *personeelachtig* to mean "staff-like" easily).
    *   It is a **lexicalized loanword**. The connection to the word *persoon* is transparent, but the suffix *-eel* is no longer productive in this specific context.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`personeel`** is a **derived noun** formed from a loaned root and a suffix. It represents a semantic shift from an adjective ("relating to people") to a collective noun ("the group of people"). While it retains the morphological structure of its French ancestor (*personnel*), it has been fully naturalized into the Dutch lexicon as a standard neuter noun.
