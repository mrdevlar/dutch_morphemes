From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`tenslotte`** is a fascinating example of **lexicalization** and **fossilization**. While it functions today as a single adverb, its internal structure reveals a historical prepositional phrase that has been grammaticalized over time.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
While modern orthography writes it as one word, morphologically and etymologically, it consists of two distinct parts:

> **`ten`** + **`slotte`**

### 2. Component Analysis

#### A. The Prefix: `ten`
*   **Origin:** This is a contraction of the preposition **`te`** (to/at) and the definite article **`den`** (the).
*   **Grammatical Case:** In Middle Dutch, the preposition *te* governed the **dative case**. Therefore, *te* + *den* (dative masculine/neuter singular) fused into *ten*.
*   **Modern Status:** In contemporary Dutch, *ten* is no longer productive as a general contraction of *te* + *den*. It survives only in fossilized phrases (e.g., *ten behoeve van*, *ten koste van*, *tenslotte*).
*   **English Cognate:** Related to the English contraction **"to the"** (though English does not fuse them in the same way).

#### B. The Root: `slotte`
*   **Root:** **`slot`**.
    *   **Meaning:** Historically, *slot* meant "lock," "bolt," or "closure." By extension, it came to mean the "end" or "conclusion" of something (the "closing" of a sequence).
    *   **Etymology:** Cognate with the English word **`slot`** (originally a notch or opening for a lock) and the verb **`sluiten`** (to close).
*   **Inflection:** The suffix **`-e`**.
    *   **Function:** This is the **dative singular ending**. Because the preposition *te* required the dative case, the noun *slot* took the inflection *-e*.
    *   **Modern Status:** Dutch has largely lost its case system. Nouns no longer take dative endings in modern speech. The *-e* in *slotte* is a **fossilized morpheme**; it is preserved only in this specific idiom.
*   **English Cognate:** Related to the English phrase **"at the end"** (where "end" corresponds to the semantic shift of *slot*).

### 3. Semantic Shift and Grammaticalization

1.  **Literal Meaning:** "At the lock" or "At the closure."
2.  **Metonymy:** The physical "closure" of a door or object was mapped onto the temporal "closure" of an event or argument.
3.  **Current Meaning:** "Finally," "lastly," or "in the end."
4.  **Part of Speech:** It has undergone **grammaticalization** from a prepositional phrase (locative) to a **sentence adverb** (temporal/logical). It is used to introduce the final point in a list or argument.

### 4. Orthographic Note
According to the official Dutch spelling rules (*Het Groene Boekje*), the word is written as one word: **`tenslotte`**.
*   **Incorrect:** *ten slotte* (two words).
*   **Correct:** *tenslotte* (one word).

This reflects its status as a single lexical unit in the modern language, even though the internal morphological boundaries remain visible to the linguist.

### 6. Comparison with English
To understand the structure, compare it to the English phrase **"at last"** or **"in the end."**
*   **Dutch:** *te* (at) + *den* (the) + *slot* (end) + *-e* (dative) $\rightarrow$ **tenslotte**.
*   **English:** *at* + *the* + *end*.

Just as English speakers do not analyze "at last" as *at* + *last* (where *last* is an adjective) in terms of case endings, Dutch speakers do not actively analyze the *-e* in *tenslotte* as a case marker, though it remains etymologically significant.
