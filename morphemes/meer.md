From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`meer`** presents a fascinating case of **homonymy**. It represents two distinct words with different meanings and etymological origins, yet they share the exact same spelling and pronunciation.

In both cases, **`meer` is a monomorphemic word**. This means it consists of a single **free morpheme** (a root word that cannot be broken down into smaller meaningful units). There are no prefixes or suffixes attached to it in its base form.

Here is the breakdown of the two distinct lexical items:

---

### 1. `meer` (Adjective/Adverb) = "More"

*   **Meaning:** Comparative degree of quantity (more).
*   **Morphological Structure:**
    *   **`meer`** (1 Free Morpheme)
*   **Analysis:**
    *   **Suppletion:** Linguistically, this is a case of **suppletion**. In Dutch, the comparative of the adjective/adverb *veel* (much/many) is not formed by adding the standard comparative suffix *-er* (i.e., it is not *veeler*). Instead, it uses a completely different root form: *meer*.
    *   **Paradigm:**
        *   Positive: *veel* (much)
        *   Comparative: *meer* (more)
        *   Superlative: *meest* (most)
    *   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*maizō**. It is cognate with the English word **more** and the German word **mehr**.
    *   **Function:** It functions as a quantifier.
    *   **Example:** *Ik heb **meer** geld.* (I have **more** money.)

### 2. `meer` (Noun) = "Lake"

*   **Meaning:** A large inland body of water.
*   **Morphological Structure:**
    *   **`meer`** (1 Free Morpheme)
*   **Analysis:**
    *   **Derivation:** This word is a noun root. It can take inflectional suffixes, such as the plural *-en*.
        *   Singular: *meer* (lake)
        *   Plural: *meren* (lakes)
    *   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*mari-**. It is cognate with the archaic/poetic English word **mere** (as in "mere water") and the Latin word **mare** (sea).
    *   **False Friend:** This is a classic "false friend" for English speakers. While English *mere* means lake, modern English *sea* corresponds to Dutch *zee*. However, in German, *Meer* means "sea."
    *   **Example:** *We zwemmen in het **meer**.* (We are swimming in the **lake**.)

### Summary of Morphological Breakdown

Because `meer` is a root word in both instances, there is no internal segmentation (like *prefix + root + suffix*).

### Why you cannot break it down further
In morphology, we look for **morphemes** (the smallest units of meaning).
*   If we tried to split it into `m` + `eer`, neither part carries meaning.
*   If we tried to split it into `me` + `er`, the `-er` is not the comparative suffix here (because the word *is* the comparative form of *veel*, but the spelling does not reflect the suffix addition).

Therefore, linguistically, **`meer` is an atomic unit** in the Dutch lexicon. To analyze it further, one must look at **etymology** (historical roots) rather than synchronic morphology (current structure).
