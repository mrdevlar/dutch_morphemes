From a linguistic perspective, the analysis of the Dutch word **`ambitie`** requires looking at it through two lenses: **synchronic** (how it functions in modern Dutch) and **diachronic** (its historical/etymological structure).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes.

### 1. Synchronic Analysis (Modern Dutch)
In contemporary Dutch morphology, **`ambitie`** is generally treated as a **loanword** (leenwoord). While it has internal structure, Dutch speakers do not actively decompose it into smaller meaningful units during speech.

*   **Morphological Status:** **Monomorphemic** (one morpheme) or **Bound Root + Suffix**.
*   **Analysis:**
    *   **`ambit-`**: A **bound root**. It carries the core lexical meaning ("striving/desire") but cannot stand alone as an independent word in Dutch.
    *   **`-ie`**: A **nominalizing suffix**. This is a very common suffix in Dutch (borrowed from French/Latin) used to turn verbs or roots into abstract nouns (e.g., *energie*, *fantasie*, *politie*).

**Why this matters:**
Because it is a loanword, it follows Dutch inflection rules despite its foreign origin.
*   **Plural:** *ambiti**es*** (adding the Dutch plural suffix *-s*).
*   **Adjective Derivation:** *ambiti**eus*** (adding the adjectival suffix *-eus*).

---

### 2. Diachronic Analysis (Etymological/Latin Roots)
To truly "break up" the morphemes, we must look at the word's origin. *Ambitie* entered Dutch via French (*ambition*) from the Latin **`ambitio`**.

In Latin, the word was morphologically transparent. Here is the deep structure:

| Morpheme | Form | Origin | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Prefix** | **`ambi-`** | Latin | "Around," "both," or "on both sides." |
| **Root** | **`-it-`** | Latin (*ire*) | "To go." (From the verb *ambire*). |
| **Suffix** | **`-io`** | Latin | Noun-forming suffix indicating an action or process. |

**The Original Construction:**
`ambi-` (around) + `ire` (to go) + `-tio` (noun) = **`ambitio`**

**Literal Meaning:** "The act of going around."

---

### 3. Semantic Shift (Meaning Evolution)
Understanding the morphemes explains the strange evolution of the word's meaning:

1.  **Literal (Latin):** *Ambire* meant literally "to walk around."
2.  **Political Context:** In Ancient Rome, politicians would literally **walk around** the marketplace or the voting districts to **canvass** for votes.
3.  **Figurative:** The act of "going around for votes" became associated with **seeking power** or **office**.
4.  **Modern:** The specific political context faded, leaving the general sense of **strong desire to achieve success** or **striving for a goal**.

### 4. Summary of Morphological Relationships in Dutch
While *ambitie* itself is a noun, its morphological family in Dutch shows how the root interacts with other affixes:

*   **`ambitie`** (Noun): The desire to achieve.
*   **`ambitieus`** (Adjective): *ambiti-* (root) + *-eus* (suffix meaning "full of" or "characterized by").
*   **`ambitieloos`** (Adjective): *ambitie* (root) + *-loos* (suffix meaning "without").
*   **`ambities`** (Plural Noun): *ambitie* (root) + *-s* (plural marker).

### Conclusion
*   **Linguistically (Dutch):** It is a **single morpheme** (or a bound root + suffix) functioning as a noun.
*   **Etymologically (Latin):** It is a compound of **`ambi-`** (around) + **`ire`** (to go) + **`-tio`** (action noun).

The word serves as a classic example of how a concrete physical action ("walking around") can morphologically and semantically evolve into an abstract psychological concept ("ambition").
