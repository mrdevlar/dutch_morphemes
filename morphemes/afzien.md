From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`afzien`** is a classic example of a **separable compound verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*). It consists of two distinct morphemes that combine to create a meaning that is often idiomatic rather than purely literal.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Structure

| Morpheme | Type | Category | Meaning/Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`af`** | Prefix / Particle | Preposition / Adverb | Away, off, down, completion |
| **`zien`** | Root | Verb | To see, to look |
| **`afzien`** | **Compound** | **Verb** | **To renounce / To overlook / To struggle** |

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Prefix: `af`
*   **Classification:** In this context, `af` functions as a **verbal particle** (specifically a separable prefix). Historically, it is a preposition or adverb.
*   **Etymology:** It stems from Old Dutch *af*, cognate with German *ab* and English *off*.
*   **Semantic Contribution:**
    *   **Direction:** It indicates movement away from a source or a position (e.g., *afvallen* = to fall off).
    *   **Completion:** It can indicate the completion of an action (e.g., *afmaken* = to finish).
    *   **Negation/Reversal:** In the context of *afzien*, it implies turning the gaze *away* from something.
*   **Grammatical Behavior:** Because `af` is a separable prefix, in a main clause, it detaches from the root verb and moves to the end of the sentence (e.g., *Ik **zie** het **af***).

#### B. The Root: `zien`
*   **Classification:** This is the **free morpheme** and the lexical root of the verb.
*   **Etymology:** It stems from Old Dutch *sēn*, cognate with German *sehen* and English *see*.
*   **Semantic Contribution:** It denotes the act of visual perception, looking, or understanding.
*   **Grammatical Behavior:** It carries the tense, person, and number inflections (e.g., *ik zie*, *jij ziet*, *wij zagen*).

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning is built)

The meaning of `afzien` is not simply the sum of "off" + "see." It has undergone **semantic shift** and **idiomatization**. There are three primary meanings derived from the combination of these morphemes:

1.  **Literal (Archaic/Rare):** "To see from a distance."
    *   *Logic:* Looking *away* or *off* into the distance.
2.  **Idiomatic (Most Common): "To renounce" or "To give up."**
    *   *Phrase:* *Afzien van iets* (to renounce something).
    *   *Logic:* You turn your gaze *away* from a claim, desire, or right. You stop "seeing" it as something you possess or want.
    *   *Example:* *Ik zie af van mijn recht.* (I renounce my right.)
3.  **Colloquial/Idiomatic: "To struggle" or "To suffer."**
    *   *Usage:* *Het is afzien.* (It is a struggle / It's hard work.)
    *   *Logic:* This implies the physical act of looking down or away due to exhaustion, or the effort required to keep going. It suggests a state of hardship where one might want to look away.

---

### 4. Morphophonological Note (The "Ge-" Insertion)

A key linguistic feature of Dutch separable verbs is visible in the **past participle**. When forming the perfect tense, the prefix `af` separates from the root `zien`, and the participle marker `ge-` is inserted **between** them.

*   **Infinitive:** `af` + `zien`
*   **Past Participle:** `af` + **`ge`** + `zien` $\rightarrow$ **`afgezien`**

This morphological rule confirms that `af` and `zien` are distinct morphological units that can be separated by other grammatical markers.

### 5. Summary
Linguistically, **`afzien`** is a **separable compound verb**.
*   **`af`** acts as a directional/negating particle.
*   **`zien`** acts as the semantic core (perception).
*   Together, they create a polysemous verb where the physical act of "looking away" metaphorically evolved into "giving up" or "renouncing."
