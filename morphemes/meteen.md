From a linguistic perspective, the analysis of the Dutch word **`meteen`** involves looking at it through two lenses: **diachronic** (historical/etymological) and **synchronic** (current usage).

While it looks like two words combined, linguistically it is a fascinating case of **lexicalization** and **fusion**.

Here is the breakdown:

### 1. Surface Morphological Breakdown
At a surface level, the word appears to consist of two distinct morphemes:

*   **`met`** (Preposition)
*   **`een`** (Numeral / Indefinite Article)

However, in modern Dutch morphology, **`meteen`** is generally analyzed as a **single, unanalyzable morpheme** (a root). It is a **lexicalized adverb**. Native speakers do not actively parse it as "with" + "one" during real-time speech processing; they retrieve it as a single unit meaning "immediately."

### 2. Diachronic Analysis (Etymology & History)
To understand the components, we must look at the history of the phrase from which it evolved.

#### A. The Morpheme `met`
*   **Origin:** Old Dutch *met*, Middle Dutch *met*.
*   **Cognates:** German *mit*, English *mid* (as in "midday"), Latin *medius*.
*   **Original Meaning:** "With" (accompaniment or instrumentality).
*   **Linguistic Note:** While English uses "with" for this concept, Dutch uses "met." The English "with" actually derives from a different Germanic root (*wiþ-*), whereas Dutch "met" derives from the root *\*mid-*.

#### B. The Morpheme `een`
*   **Origin:** Old Dutch *ēn*, Middle Dutch *een*.
*   **Cognates:** English *one*, German *ein*.
*   **Original Meaning:** The number "one" or the indefinite article "a/an."

#### C. The Original Phrase: *Met een*
Historically, this was a prepositional phrase meaning "with one." It was almost always followed by an implied noun, usually *slag* (stroke/blow) or *keer* (time/turn).
*   *Full phrase:* "Met één [slag/keer]" (With one [stroke/turn]).
*   *Meaning:* "In a single attempt" or "In one go."

### 3. Semantic Shift (How meaning changed)
The transition from a literal phrase to an adverb of time is a classic example of **semantic bleaching** and **ellipsis**.

1.  **Ellipsis:** The noun following "met een" was dropped because it was understood from context.
    *   *Example:* "Ik doe het met een." (I'll do it with one [stroke].) $\rightarrow$ "Ik doe het **meteen**."
2.  **Metonymy:** The concept of "doing something in one stroke" shifted to mean "without delay." If you do something in one stroke, there is no pause between the intention and the action.
3.  **Fusion:** Over centuries, the two words merged into one orthographic unit (`meteen`) and became a fixed adverb.

### 4. Synchronic Analysis (Current Linguistic Status)
In modern Dutch linguistics, `meteen` is treated as follows:

*   **Word Class:** Adverb (*Bijwoord*).
*   **Morphological Status:** **Monomorphemic** (one morpheme). Even though it looks like it has two parts, the internal structure is no longer productive. You cannot create new words using this pattern (e.g., you cannot say `mettwee` to mean "immediately" or "in two").
*   **Orthography:** It is written as one word. Writing it as two words (`met een`) changes the meaning entirely to the literal prepositional phrase (e.g., *Ik ga met een auto* = "I am going with a car").

### 5. Cross-Linguistic Comparison
This phenomenon is common in many languages, where a phrase fuses into a single adverb:

*   **English:** *Straightaway* (Straight + Away), *Right now* (often treated as a single temporal unit), *Withal* (With + All).
*   **German:** *Gleich* (originally related to "equal," now "immediately"), *Sofort* (originally "so forth").
*   **French:** *Tout de suite* (literally "all of step," now "immediately").

### Conclusion
While you can physically break `meteen` into **`met`** and **`een`**, linguistically it is a **fossilized compound**. It represents a historical phrase ("with one") that has undergone **lexicalization**, losing its internal grammatical structure to become a single, atomic adverb meaning "immediately."
