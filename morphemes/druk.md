From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`druk`** is analyzed as a **monomorphemic** word. This means it consists of a single morpheme.

Here is the detailed breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Structure
*   **Word:** `druk`
*   **Morpheme Count:** 1
*   **Morpheme Type:** **Free Root Morpheme**
*   **Breakdown:** `[druk]`

Unlike complex words (e.g., *druk-ker-ij* [print-shop] or *on-druk-ke-lijk* [unprintable]), `druk` contains no prefixes, suffixes, or infixes. It is the lexical base itself.

### 2. Syntactic and Semantic Function (Polysemy)
Although it is a single morpheme, `druk` is **polysemous**, meaning it carries multiple related meanings depending on its syntactic category (part of speech).

*   **As a Noun (*de druk*):**
    *   **Meaning:** Pressure, stress, or print.
    *   **Example:** *De druk op de knop.* (The pressure on the button.)
    *   **Example:** *In druk.* (In print.)
*   **As an Adjective (*druk*):**
    *   **Meaning:** Busy, crowded, or noisy.
    *   **Example:** *Een drukke dag.* (A busy day.)
    *   *Note:* When used attributively before a noun, it often takes the inflectional suffix *-e* (becoming *drukke*), but the root remains `druk`.
*   **As a Verb Stem (*drukken*):**
    *   While the infinitive verb is *drukken* (to press/print), `druk` serves as the **stem** (or root) to which the verbal inflection *-en* is attached.

While `druk` is a single morpheme in isolation, it is highly productive in Dutch morphology. It serves as the base for many derived words. This demonstrates that `druk` is a **bound** or **free** root depending on usage, but primarily functions as a **free root** in this specific instance.

From a morphological standpoint, **`druk`** is a **monomorphemic free root**. It does not break down further into smaller meaningful units.
