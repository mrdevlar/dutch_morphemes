From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch verb **`verrassen`** (to surprise) can be analyzed through its morphology (structure), phonology (sound), and etymology (history).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word is composed of three distinct morphological units:

> **ver-** + **ras** + **-en**

*   **ver-**: Prefix
*   **ras**: Root (Stem)
*   **-en**: Infinitive Suffix

---

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Prefix: `ver-`
*   **Type:** Inseparable Prefix (*onlosmakelijk voorvoegsel*).
*   **Function:** In Dutch, the prefix *ver-* often indicates a change of state, a transformation, or a completion of an action. It can also imply a negative or intensive aspect (e.g., *verlies* from *lijden*, *verbranden* from *branden*).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *\*far-*, related to the English prefix *for-* (as in *forbid*) and German *ver-*.
*   **Syntax:** Crucially, *ver-* is an **inseparable** prefix. This means:
    *   The stress falls on the root, not the prefix (ver**ras**sen).
    *   It does not take the *ge-* prefix in the past participle (e.g., *verrast*, not *geverrast*).
    *   It cannot be separated from the verb in a sentence (unlike separable verbs like *opbellen*).

#### B. The Root: `ras`
*   **Type:** Bound Root.
*   **Meaning:** This root is related to speed, rushing, or sudden movement. It is cognate with the Dutch verb **`rasen`** (to rage, to rush, to speed) and the English adjective **`rash`** (acting with speed/impulsiveness).
*   **Semantic Shift:** Historically, the meaning was "to rush upon someone." Over time, this evolved into "to catch someone off guard while rushing," which semantically shifted to the modern meaning: **"to surprise."**
*   **Distinction:** It is important not to confuse this root with the noun **`ras`** (race/breed). While they look identical in modern Dutch, they have different etymological origins. The *ras* in *verrassen* is about speed; the *ras* in *hondenras* (dog breed) is about lineage.

#### C. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Infinitive Marker.
*   **Function:** This is the standard ending for Dutch verbs in the infinitive form (equivalent to English *-ing* or the bare infinitive *to* + verb).
*   **Phonology:** In spoken Dutch, this is often reduced to a schwa sound /ə/ or dropped entirely in casual speech (e.g., *ik ga verras*).

---

### 3. Orthography and Phonology (The "Double S")
You may notice the spelling contains a double **`ss`** (*verrassen*), but the morphological root is just **`ras`**.

*   **The Rule:** Dutch orthography follows a strict rule regarding vowel length. A short vowel in a stressed syllable must be followed by a double consonant to indicate that the vowel remains short.
*   **Analysis:**
    *   Root: /ras/ (Short 'a' sound).
    *   Suffix: /ən/
    *   If spelled *verrasen*, the 'a' might be interpreted as long (like in *na*).
    *   To preserve the short /a/ sound, the 's' is doubled: **`rass`**.
*   **Linguistic Note:** The double 's' is **not** a separate morpheme; it is an orthographic device to preserve the phonological quality of the root vowel.

### 5. Semantic Evolution
The evolution of the meaning is a classic example of **metonymy** (where an action is named after the circumstances surrounding it):

1.  **Literal:** To rush upon someone (*rasen* + *ver-*).
2.  **Contextual:** Catching someone while they are unprepared because you rushed them.
3.  **Modern:** To surprise someone (the element of "rushing" is metaphorical; the focus is on the unexpectedness).

### 6. Comparison with German
For linguistic context, the German cognate is **`überraschen`**.
*   Dutch uses the prefix **`ver-`** (change/completion).
*   German uses the prefix **`über-`** (over/across).
*   Both share the same root concept of "surprise" derived from sudden movement.
