From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`luidruchtig`** (meaning *noisy*, *loud*, or *clamorous*) is a complex word formed through derivation and compounding.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Visual Segmentation
The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

> **luid** + **rucht** + **ig**

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

| Morpheme | Type | Part of Speech (Base) | Meaning / Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **luid** | **Free Morpheme** | Adjective | "Loud" |
| **rucht** | **Bound Morpheme** | Noun Root | "Noise", "Rumor", "Report" |
| **-ig** | **Bound Morpheme** | Derivational Suffix | "Having the quality of", "-y", "-ish" |

### 3. Detailed Explanation

#### **A. `luid` (Root 1)**
*   **Status:** Free morpheme.
*   **Function:** This is a standalone adjective in modern Dutch (e.g., *een luid geluid* / a loud sound).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *lût*, Old High German *lūt*. It is cognate with the English word **loud** and the German word **laut**.
*   **Role in Compound:** It acts as the modifier within the compound base, describing the type of "noise."

#### **B. `rucht` (Root 2)**
*   **Status:** Bound morpheme (in modern Dutch).
*   **Function:** This is a nominal root. In contemporary Dutch, *rucht* does not exist as a standalone word. You will typically find it in the word **`gerucht`** (rumor/noise), where the prefix *ge-* nominalizes the root.
*   **Etymology:** Related to the verb *roepen* (to call) or the Middle Dutch *ruchte*. Cognate with German *Gerücht* and English **rumor** (via French *rumeur*, though the Germanic root is shared).
*   **Role in Compound:** It serves as the head noun of the internal compound *luidrucht* (literally "loud-noise").

#### **C. `-ig` (Suffix)**
*   **Status:** Bound morpheme (Derivational Suffix).
*   **Function:** This is a highly productive adjectival suffix in Dutch. It attaches to nouns (or nominal compounds) to create adjectives meaning "characterized by" or "full of."
*   **Etymology:** From Middle Dutch *-ich*, Old High German *-ag*. It is cognate with the English suffix **-y** (as in *windy* or *noisy*) and the German suffix **-ig** (as in *lautig* - though *laut* is usually used directly, *laut* + *ig* exists in dialects, standard German uses *laut* as adj).
*   **Role in Compound:** It converts the nominal concept *luidrucht* into an adjective describing a person or thing that possesses that quality.

### 4. Structural Synthesis

The morphological structure follows this hierarchy:

1.  **Compounding:** `luid` (Adj) + `rucht` (Noun Root) $\rightarrow$ **`luidrucht`** (Nominal Compound concept: "loud noise").
2.  **Derivation:** `luidrucht` (Noun) + `-ig` (Suffix) $\rightarrow$ **`luidruchtig`** (Adjective).

**Semantic Composition:**
*   *Luid* (Loud) + *Rucht* (Noise/Rumor) + *-ig* (Having the quality of)
*   **Literal:** "Having the quality of loud noise."
*   **Actual Meaning:** Noisy, clamorous, vociferous.

### 5. Linguistic Notes

*   **Stress Pattern:** In Dutch compounds, the primary stress usually falls on the first element.
    *   Pronunciation: **`LUID`**-ruch-tig (/ˈlœy̯t.rʏx.təx/).
    *   This confirms that *luid* is the semantic head of the internal compound, distinguishing it from the suffix *-ig*, which is unstressed.
*   **Lexicalization:** While the components are analyzable, the word *luidruchtig* is lexicalized. A speaker does not need to actively construct the meaning every time; it is stored in the mental lexicon as a single unit meaning "noisy."
*   **Linking Elements:** Dutch compounds often use linking sounds (like *-s-* or *-e-*). In this specific case, there is **no linking element** between *luid* and *rucht*. They are joined directly.
