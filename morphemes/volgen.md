From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`volgen`** is a verb in its infinitive form. To analyze its morphology, we must distinguish between its grammatical structure (how it is built for the sentence) and its etymological history (where the sounds come from).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes in **`volgen`**.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two morphemes:

> **`volg`** + **`-en`**

| Morpheme | Type | Function | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`volg-`** | **Root (Stem)** | Lexical | The core semantic meaning ("to follow"). |
| **`-en`** | **Suffix** | Inflectional | Marks the verb as an **infinitive**. |

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `volg-`
*   **Status:** This is a **free morpheme** in the sense that it carries the independent lexical meaning, though in modern Dutch it cannot stand alone as a word (you cannot say "Ik volg" without a subject, and the bare stem is not used in isolation).
*   **Verb Class:** It is a **weak verb** (regular verb).
*   **Phonology:** The `g` in Dutch is typically a voiced velar fricative [ɣ] or a voiceless velar fricative [x] depending on the dialect and position. In *volgen*, it is pronounced clearly.
*   **Stability:** This root remains stable across most conjugations:
    *   *Ik volg* (I follow)
    *   *Jij volgt* (You follow)
    *   *Ik volgde* (I followed)
    *   *Gevolgd* (Followed - past participle)

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Status:** This is a **bound morpheme**. It cannot exist without the root.
*   **Function:** It is the standard **infinitive marker** for Dutch verbs.
*   **Comparison:**
    *   In English, the infinitive marker is the particle *to* (e.g., *to follow*), and the verb form is bare (*follow*).
    *   In Dutch, the infinitive is marked by the suffix *-en* attached directly to the stem (*volg* + *en*).
    *   This is cognate with the Old English infinitive ending *-an* (e.g., *folgian* > *follow*).

### 3. Etymological Perspective (Historical Morphology)
While the modern breakdown is simply Root + Suffix, looking at the history reveals why the word looks the way it does.

*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*fulganą*
*   **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** *\*pel-* (meaning "to drive, push, lead").
    *   This is the same root that gives us English **follow**, German **folgen**, and Latin **pellicere** (to draw in).
*   **False Friend Warning:**
    *   Modern Dutch has an adjective **`vol`** meaning "full" (e.g., *een volle bus* = a full bus).
    *   However, in **`volgen`**, the syllable *vol-* is **not** the adjective *vol*. It is an indivisible part of the historical root.
    *   *Linguistic Trap:* Do not analyze `volgen` as `vol` (full) + `gen`. This is a "folk etymology" error. The semantic link between "full" and "follow" was lost centuries ago.

To understand the root `volg-` better, it helps to see how it interacts with other morphemes in derived words:

From a synchronic (current state) linguistic perspective, **`volgen`** is a **monomorphemic lexical root** (`volg-`) combined with a **grammatical inflectional suffix** (`-en`).

*   **`volg-`**: Semantic core (to follow).
*   **`-en`**: Grammatical marker (infinitive).
