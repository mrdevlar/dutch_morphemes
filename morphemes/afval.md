From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`afval`** is a morphologically transparent compound. It consists of two distinct morphemes that combine to create a specific semantic meaning.

Here is the breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:
> **`af`** + **`val`**

*   **`af`**: Prefix / Adverbial Particle
*   **`val`**: Root / Verb Stem

---

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### **Morpheme 1: `af-`**
*   **Type:** Separable Prefix / Prepositional Adverb.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *af*, cognate with German *ab* and English *off*.
*   **Proto-Germanic Root:** *\*af-* (meaning "off," "away," "down").
*   **Function:** In Dutch morphology, this prefix indicates **separation**, **direction away from a source**, or **removal**.
*   **Semantic Contribution:** It modifies the root to imply that something is being taken away, dropped off, or separated from a main body.

#### **Morpheme 2: `-val`**
*   **Type:** Nominalized Verb Stem.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Dutch verb **`vallen`** (to fall).
*   **Proto-Germanic Root:** *\*fallaną* (cognate with English *fall* and German *Fall*).
*   **Function:** This is the stem of the verb *vallen*. In this compound, it functions as a noun (nominalization).
*   **Semantic Contribution:** It refers to the act or result of falling.
*   **Note:** It is important to distinguish this from the unrelated Dutch noun *val* (meaning "trap" or "fall" in a specific context like a trap), which comes from a different root. In *afval*, it is strictly related to the motion of falling.

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning is built)

When combined, the morphemes create a literal meaning that has undergone **semantic broadening**:

1.  **Literal Meaning:** "Off-fall" or "That which falls away."
2.  **Original Concept:** Historically, this referred to physical debris, leaves, or parts that literally fell off a tree, a building, or a person.
3.  **Modern Meaning:** Through semantic shift, it now encompasses **all types of waste, rubbish, trash, or refuse**. It implies material that has been discarded or separated from what is useful.

**Linguistic Process:** This is an example of **metonymy**, where the physical action (falling off) represents the resulting object (the trash).

---

### 4. Cross-Linguistic Cognates and Comparisons

To understand the morphology better, it helps to look at related words in other Germanic languages:

*   **German:** *Abfall* (Identical structure: *ab-* + *Fall*). Meaning: Waste/Trash.
*   **English:** *Offal*.
    *   *Linguistic Note:* This is a direct cognate (*off* + *fall*). However, in English, the meaning narrowed specifically to "animal entrails/organs" (by-products of slaughter), whereas Dutch *afval* and German *Abfall* retained the broader meaning of general waste.
*   **English:** *Fall-off*.
    *   *Linguistic Note:* This is a calque (literal translation) of the Dutch structure, but it is not used as a noun for "trash."

### 5. Related Derivations

The morphological structure of *afval* connects to a family of related words in Dutch:

*   **`afvallen` (Verb):** To fall off; to lose weight (literally "to fall off" in terms of mass).
    *   *Morphology:* `af` (prefix) + `vallen` (verb).
*   **`afvalwater` (Compound Noun):** Wastewater.
    *   *Morphology:* `afval` (waste) + `water` (water).
*   **`afvalbak` (Compound Noun):** Trash can.
    *   *Morphology:* `afval` (waste) + `bak` (bin/bowl).
