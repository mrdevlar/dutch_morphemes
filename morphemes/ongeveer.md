From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`ongeveer`** (meaning "approximately," "about," or "roughly") is a fascinating example of **derivational morphology** combined with **semantic shift** and **lexicalization**.

While modern speakers treat it as a single unit, it can be broken down into three distinct morphological components based on its historical development.

Here is the breakdown:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
**`on-`** + **`ge-`** + **`veer`**

| Morpheme | Type | Function | Origin/Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`on-`** | Prefix | Negation / Privative | Cognate with English *un-* or *non-*. |
| **`ge-`** | Prefix | Nominalizer | Forms nouns from verbs/adjectives (archaic). |
| **`veer`** | Root | Semantic Core | An older form of *vaar* (related to *gevaar*). |

---

### 2. Detailed Explanation of Morphemes

#### **A. `on-` (The Negative Prefix)**
*   **Function:** This is a **privative prefix**. It negates the meaning of the base word to which it is attached.
*   **Comparison:** It is directly cognate with the English prefix **`un-`** (as in *untrue*, *unhappy*) and the German **`un-`**.
*   **Meaning in this context:** It implies "without" or "lacking."

#### **B. `ge-` (The Nominalizing Prefix)**
*   **Function:** In older Dutch (Middle Dutch), **`ge-`** was a productive prefix used to form nouns from verbs or adjectives.
*   **Modern Status:** In modern Dutch, this prefix is often fossilized. You see it in words like *gebruik* (use), *gevaar* (danger), or *gezicht* (face). It is no longer productive (you cannot simply add *ge-* to any verb to make a noun today).
*   **In `ongeveer`:** This *ge-* is part of the archaic root structure. It does not carry its modern verbal participle function (like in *gegeten*).

#### **C. `veer` (The Root)**
*   **Etymology:** This is the most complex part. The root **`veer`** is **not** related to the modern Dutch word *veer* (meaning "spring" or "feather").
*   **Historical Root:** It is an older phonetic variant of **`vaar`**, which is the root of the modern word **`gevaar`** (danger/risk).
*   **Phonological Shift:** In Middle Dutch, the word was *ongheveer*. Over time, the vowel in the root shifted from *a* (as in *gevaar*) to *e* (as in *ongeveer*). This is a common type of vowel alternation in Germanic languages.

---

### 3. Etymology and Semantic Shift
To understand the word fully, we must look at how the meaning changed over time (semantic shift).

1.  **Original Meaning (Middle Dutch):**
    The word was constructed as **`on-`** + **`gevaar`** (without danger).
    *   *Literal meaning:* "Without risk" or "Safe."
    *   *Context:* If you did something "without danger," you were not risking a specific outcome.

2.  **Semantic Shift:**
    The meaning evolved from "without physical danger" to "without risk of being wrong."
    *   If you state a number "without risk of being exact," you are stating it **approximately**.
    *   This is similar to the English phrase "to play it safe" evolving into "to be vague."

3.  **Modern Meaning:**
    Today, the connection to "danger" is completely lost. The word is **lexicalized**, meaning it functions as a single adverb meaning "approximately."
    *   *Example:* "Het duurt **ongeveer** een uur." (It takes **approximately** an hour.)

### 4. Linguistic Classification
*   **Word Class:** Adverb (bijwoord).
*   **Morphological Type:** **Derivational Compound**. It is formed by adding a prefix to a base, but the base itself is a fossilized compound.
*   **Productivity:** **Non-productive**. You cannot create new words using this pattern (e.g., you cannot say *ongezomer* to mean "approximately summer").
*   **Stress Pattern:** The stress falls on the final syllable: *on-ge-**veer*** (/ɔn.ɣəˈver/). This is typical for Dutch words where the prefix *on-* is attached to a base that already carries stress on the final syllable (like *gevaar*).

### Summary
While a modern Dutch speaker sees **`ongeveer`** as one word, a linguist sees a historical fossil:
> **"Without" (`on-`) + "Risk/Danger" (`ge-` + `veer`)**
> $\rightarrow$ **"Without risk of being exact"**
> $\rightarrow$ **"Approximately"**
