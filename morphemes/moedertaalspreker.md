From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`moedertaalspreker`** is a complex noun formed through compounding and derivation. It is a classic example of Dutch agglutinative tendencies, where multiple meaningful units (morphemes) are strung together to create a specific concept.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Visual Segmentation
To understand the structure, we can segment the word into its constituent morphemes:

> **moeder** + **taal** + **s** + **sprek** + **er**

### 2. Morpheme-by-Morpheme Analysis

#### A. `moeder` (Root / Free Morpheme)
*   **Category:** Noun (Lexical Root).
*   **Meaning:** "Mother."
*   **Function:** In this compound, it acts as a **modifier**. It specifies the type of *taal* (language).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *\*mōdēr*, cognate with English *mother* and German *Mutter*.
*   **Linguistic Note:** This is a **free morpheme**, meaning it can stand alone as an independent word.

#### B. `taal` (Root / Free Morpheme)
*   **Category:** Noun (Lexical Root).
*   **Meaning:** "Language" or "Tongue."
*   **Function:** This is the **head** of the first compound unit (*moedertaal*). The meaning of the compound is determined by this word (it is a type of language).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *thioda* (people/nation), related to the concept of "the people's speech." Cognate with English *deed* (in the sense of "people's language" -> *Deutsch*) and German *Deutsch*.
*   **Linguistic Note:** Also a **free morpheme**.

#### C. `s` (Linking Morpheme / Interfix)
*   **Category:** Bound Morpheme (Linking Element).
*   **Meaning:** No independent lexical meaning.
*   **Function:** This is a **linking 's'** (voegsel). Historically, this represents a genitive marker (possessive 's), indicating that the *spreker* belongs to or is associated with the *moedertaal*. In modern Dutch morphology, it is analyzed as a linking element that facilitates the phonological and morphological connection between two nouns in a compound.
*   **Linguistic Note:** This is a **bound morpheme**; it cannot stand alone.

#### D. `sprek` (Stem / Bound Morpheme)
*   **Category:** Verb Stem.
*   **Meaning:** "Speak."
*   **Function:** This is the root of the verb *spreken* (to speak). In Dutch compounding, the verb is reduced to its stem form.
*   **Linguistic Note:** This is a **bound morpheme** in this context. While the verb *spreken* is a free word, the stem *sprek* only appears here as part of a larger structure.

#### E. `-er` (Derivational Suffix)
*   **Category:** Suffix (Agentive).
*   **Meaning:** "One who [verb]s."
*   **Function:** This is an **agentive suffix**. It nominalizes the verb stem *sprek*, turning the action "to speak" into the agent "speaker."
*   **Etymology:** Cognate with the English agentive suffix *-er* (e.g., *teach* → *teacher*) and German *-er* (e.g., *sprechen* → *Sprecher*).
*   **Linguistic Note:** This is a **derivational morpheme** because it changes the part of speech (from verb to noun).

### 3. Hierarchical Structure
Linguists often visualize the structure as a tree to show how the word is built up layer by layer.

1.  **Level 1 (Derivation):** `sprek` + `-er` → **`spreker`** (Speaker)
2.  **Level 2 (Compounding):** `moeder` + `taal` → **`moedertaal`** (Mother tongue)
3.  **Level 3 (Compounding):** `moedertaal` + `s` + `spreker` → **`moedertaalspreker`** (Native speaker)

**Syntactic Relationship:**
*   **Head:** The final element, *spreker*, is the head of the entire word. The word refers to a *person* (a speaker), not a language.
*   **Modifier:** The first part, *moedertaal*, modifies the head. It specifies *what kind* of speaker they are.

### 4. Comparison with English
The Dutch word is a direct calque (loan translation) of the English term **"native speaker,"** but it is constructed more transparently using Germanic roots.

*   **English:** *Native* (Adjective) + *Speaker* (Noun).
*   **Dutch:** *Mother* (Noun) + *Tongue* (Noun) + *Speaker* (Noun).

While English uses an adjective (*native*) to describe the origin of the language, Dutch uses a compound noun (*moedertaal*) to describe the language itself, which then modifies the speaker.
