From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`legende`** (meaning "legend") can be analyzed on two levels: **synchronic** (how it functions in modern Dutch) and **diachronic** (its historical etymological structure).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes.

### 1. Synchronic Analysis (Modern Dutch)
In modern Dutch morphology, the word is treated as a noun (*zelfstandig naamwoord*). While many speakers perceive it as a single unit, the plural form reveals its internal structure.

*   **Word:** `legende`
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun (common gender, *de-woord*)
*   **Plural:** `legenden`

**Morpheme Breakdown:**
1.  **`legend-` (Stem/Root):** This is the bound lexical base. You cannot use this stem in isolation in modern Dutch (e.g., you cannot say *"Ik heb een legend gezien"*). It carries the core semantic meaning of "legend."
2.  **`-e` (Suffix):** This is the **singular inflectional ending**.
    *   *Note:* In Dutch, most nouns do not have a singular suffix (e.g., *de hond*, *de kat*). However, many loanwords from Latin or French ending in *-e* retain this vowel in the singular.
    *   *Evidence:* The plural form is *legenden*. If the stem were *legende*, the plural would theoretically be *legenden* (adding *-n*), but the vowel change suggests the stem is *legend-*.
    *   *Structure:* `legend-` + `-e` (Singular) vs. `legend-` + `-en` (Plural).

**Phonology:**
*   **IPA:** `/ˈleː.ɣən.də/`
*   The final `-e` is pronounced as a schwa `/ə/` (a neutral, unstressed vowel sound).

### 2. Diachronic Analysis (Etymology)
To understand *why* the stem looks like `legend-`, we must look at the word's origin. It is a loanword from Latin, entering Dutch via Medieval Latin and French.

**Source:** Latin *legenda* (plural of *legendum*).

**Morpheme Breakdown (Latin Origin):**
1.  **`leg-` (Root):** From the Latin verb *legere*, meaning "to read" or "to gather."
2.  **`-end-` (Suffix):** This is a **gerundive suffix**. In Latin grammar, the gerundive expresses necessity or obligation (passive periphrastic). It turns the verb into an adjective meaning "to be [verb]ed."
    *   *Legere* (to read) + *-nd-* = *Legendum* ("that which is to be read").
3.  **`-a` (Suffix):** A feminine noun ending in Latin. In the transition to Dutch (and Romance languages like French *légende*), the Latin `-a` evolved into the Dutch `-e`.

**Semantic Shift:**
*   **Original Meaning:** "Things to be read." Specifically, in the Middle Ages, this referred to the lives of saints read aloud in church during liturgy.
*   **Modern Meaning:** Over time, the meaning broadened from "sacred stories to be read" to "traditional stories regarded as historical but unverified," and finally to the modern sense of a "legend" (a story involving supernatural elements or exaggeration).

---

### 3. Important Linguistic Distinctions

#### A. Cognate with English
The Dutch `legende` is a **cognate** with the English word **`legend`**.
*   Both share the same Latin root (*legere*).
*   Both underwent the same semantic shift from "saint's life" to "mythical story."
*   The Dutch `-e` corresponds to the English null ending in the singular (though English *legends* adds *-s* for plural).

#### B. False Friend: `leggen`
It is crucial not to confuse `legende` with the Dutch verb **`leggen`** (to lay/place).
*   **`legende`** comes from Latin *legere* (to read).
*   **`leggen`** comes from Proto-Germanic *\*lagjan* (to lay).
*   While they look similar, they are **etymologically unrelated**. This is a classic example of a false friend within the same language family (Germanic vs. Latin loan).
