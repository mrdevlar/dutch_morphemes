From a linguistic perspective, specifically within **synchronic morphology** (the study of the structure of words in the language as it exists today), the Dutch word **`konijn`** is analyzed as follows:

### 1. Morphological Breakdown
In Modern Dutch, **`konijn`** is a **monomorphemic** word. It consists of a single free morpheme.

*   **Word:** `konijn`
*   **Morphemes:** `{konijn}`
*   **Type:** Lexical Morpheme (Root)
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun (Common gender)

There are no prefixes or suffixes attached to this word in its base form. It is a **root word**.

### 2. Synchronic vs. Diachronic Analysis
To fully understand the morphology, one must distinguish between how the word functions *now* (synchronic) and where it came from (diachronic).

#### Synchronic (Current State)
In the current Dutch language system, the sequence of letters `k-o-n-i-j-n` cannot be segmented further.
*   If you try to remove the `-ijn`, you are left with `kon`, which is not a word.
*   If you try to remove the `k-`, you are left with `onijn`, which is not a word.
*   Therefore, the entire string is the **stem**.

We know the stem is `konijn-` because of how it behaves when inflected:
*   **Plural:** `konijn` + `-en` = `konijnen` (rabbits)
*   **Diminutive:** `konijn` + `-tje` = `konijntje` (little rabbit)

In both cases, the `n` remains part of the root, confirming that `-ijn` is not a suffix in this specific word.

#### Diachronic (Historical Origin)
While it is one morpheme today, the word has a complex history that explains why it *looks* like it might have a suffix.
*   **Origin:** Latin *cuniculus* (rabbit/hole).
*   **Transmission:** Latin $\rightarrow$ Old French *conin* $\rightarrow$ Middle Dutch *conijn*.
*   **Phonological Adaptation:** When the word entered Dutch, the final sound was adapted to fit Dutch phonotactics. The French ending was reanalyzed by Dutch speakers as the Dutch spelling `-ijn`.

Because the ending `-ijn` appears in other Dutch words (often as a suffix in loanwords or derived nouns), it creates a "false segmentation" illusion. However, in `konijn`, this ending is **lexicalized**, meaning it has become an inseparable part of the root meaning.

### 3. Common Linguistic Pitfalls
A linguistic analysis must address why this word is often confusing to learners or even native speakers.

#### A. The "King" Confusion (`Koning` vs. `Konijn`)
Visually, `konijn` looks very similar to `koning` (king).
*   **`Koning`** (King): Germanic root (*kuningaz*).
*   **`Konijn`** (Rabbit): Romance/Latin root (*cuniculus*).
*   **Morphological Trap:** In `koning`, the suffix `-in` creates the female form `koningin` (queen). Because `konijn` ends in `-ijn`, learners often mistakenly assume it is a feminine form of a word like `koning`. **This is incorrect.** They are etymologically unrelated.

#### B. The Diminutive Suffix Confusion
In Dutch, the suffix `-je` (or `-tje`) creates diminutives.
*   *Huis* (house) $\rightarrow$ *Huisje*
*   *Konijn* (rabbit) $\rightarrow$ *Konijntje*
*   Some might mistakenly analyze `konijn` as `kon` + `ijn` (where `-ijn` is a diminutive). This is false. The diminutive suffix is added *after* the full root `konijn`.
