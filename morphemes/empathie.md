From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`empathie`** is a **loanword** (specifically an internationalism) derived from Greek via German and English. It is a **derived word** consisting of three distinct morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into three morphemes:
> **em- + path + -ie**

### 2. Etymological Context
To understand the morphology, one must look at the etymology. The word entered Dutch in the early 20th century (around 1910–1920) from the German *Empathie*, which was a translation of the English *empathy*. The English term was coined in 1909 by psychologist Edward Titchener as a translation of the German *Einfühlung* ("feeling into"), which itself was a calque of the Greek **`empathia`**.

### 3. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. Prefix: `em-`
*   **Type:** Bound derivational prefix.
*   **Origin:** Greek **`en-`** (ἐν), meaning "in," "into," or "within."
*   **Phonological Note:** In the transition from Greek to Latin and subsequently to modern European languages, the nasal consonant **`n`** underwent **regressive assimilation**. Because the following root begins with the bilabial stop **`p`**, the alveolar nasal **`n`** changed to the bilabial nasal **`m`** to match the place of articulation.
    *   *en-* + *path-* $\rightarrow$ **`em-`**
*   **Semantic Function:** Indicates direction or location (feeling *into* or *within* another).

#### B. Root: `path` (or `path-`)
*   **Type:** Bound root (combining form).
*   **Origin:** Greek **`pathos`** (πάθος), meaning "feeling," "suffering," "experience," or "emotion."
*   **Note on Spelling:** The **`h`** is retained in Dutch spelling to preserve the etymological link to the Greek *ph* (phi), which represents an aspirated /p/ sound in Ancient Greek. In Dutch phonology, it is pronounced as a voiceless bilabial plosive /p/.
*   **Semantic Function:** Carries the core lexical meaning related to emotion, sensation, or suffering.
*   **Productivity:** This root is highly productive in Dutch and appears in many other loanwords, such as:
    *   *sympathie* (syn- + path- + -ie)
    *   *antipathie* (anti- + path- + -ie)
    *   *pathologie* (path- + -ologie)
    *   *medelijden* (Native Dutch equivalent: *mede-* + *lijden*, where *lijden* relates to the same semantic field as *pathos*).

#### C. Suffix: `-ie`
*   **Type:** Derivational suffix (nominalizer).
*   **Origin:** Greek **`-ia`** (via Latin *-ia* and French *-ie*).
*   **Function:** Converts the verbal/adjectival root into an **abstract noun**. It denotes a state, condition, quality, or process.
*   **Pronunciation:** In Dutch, this is pronounced as a long close front unrounded vowel /i/ (like the 'ee' in 'see').
*   **Semantic Function:** Turns the concept of "feeling into" into the abstract concept of "the state of feeling into."

### 4. Semantic Synthesis
When combined, the morphemes create the following semantic structure:

1.  **`em-`**: In/Into
2.  **`path`**: Feeling/Suffering
3.  **`-ie`**: State/Condition

**Literal Meaning:** "The state of feeling into [another's experience]."

### 5. Linguistic Comparison: Native vs. Loan
From a morphological typology perspective, Dutch offers a native alternative to this loanword: **`medegevoel`**.
*   **`mede-`**: Prefix meaning "with" or "together" (cognate with English *mid-* or *with-*).
*   **`ge-`**: Prefix indicating a collective or result.
*   **`voel`**: Root from the verb *voelen* (to feel).
*   **`-`**: (No suffix, the noun is formed by the compound).

While *medegevoel* is morphologically Germanic (compound), *empathie* is morphologically Greco-Romance (derived). In modern Dutch, *empathie* is often preferred in psychological or formal contexts, whereas *medegevoel* is more colloquial.
