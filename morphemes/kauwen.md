From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`kauwen`** (meaning "to chew") is a verb consisting of two distinct morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown and analysis:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:

> **`kauw`** + **`-en`**

*   **`kauw`**: Lexical Root (Stem)
*   **`-en`**: Inflectional Suffix (Infinitive Marker)

---

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Root: `kauw-`
*   **Type:** Free Lexical Morpheme (Root).
*   **Function:** Carries the core semantic meaning of the word.
*   **Meaning:** "To chew" or "masticate."
*   **Part of Speech:** Verbal Stem.
*   **Etymology & Cognates:**
    *   **Proto-Germanic:** Reconstructed as **\*kauwōną**.
    *   **Old Dutch:** *kauwōn*.
    *   **Middle Dutch:** *kauwen*.
    *   **Cognates:**
        *   **German:** *kauen* (to chew).
        *   **English:** *chew* (Old English *ceowan*). Note the consonant shift: The Proto-Germanic *\*k-* remained a *k* in Dutch and German, but shifted to *ch* (/tʃ/) in English due to the High German Consonant Shift (which also affected English via West Germanic dialects).
        *   **Frisian:** *kjeie*.
*   **Phonology:** In standard Dutch, the diphthong *au* is pronounced /ɑu̯/. The final *w* is a voiced labio-velar approximant /ʋ/.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Morpheme.
*   **Function:** Marks the verb as an **infinitive** (the dictionary form).
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, almost all verbs take the suffix *-en* in the infinitive form (e.g., *lopen*, *maken*, *zien* → *zien* is irregular but ends in -en phonologically, *eten* → *eten*).
*   **Phonology:** In the infinitive form, this suffix is typically pronounced as a schwa /ə/ followed by a nasal /n/, though in rapid speech the /n/ may be elided or nasalize the preceding vowel depending on the dialect.
*   **Polysemy of the Suffix:** While here it marks the infinitive, this same suffix *-en* appears in other grammatical contexts:
    *   **Plural Present Tense:** *Wij kauw**en*** (We chew).
    *   **Plural Nouns:** *Hond**en*** (Dogs).
    *   **Adjective Inflection:** *Een klein**e** hond* vs. *De klein**e** hond* (Note: Adjectives usually take *-e*, but *-en* is used in specific plural/weak contexts).
    *   *In this specific word (`kauwen`), it is strictly the infinitive marker.*

---

### 3. Syntactic and Inflectional Behavior
To understand the root `kauw-` fully, it helps to look at how it behaves in other inflected forms. This confirms that `kauw-` is a stable stem.

*   **Present Tense (Singular):** *Ik kauw* (I chew) — The suffix is dropped.
*   **Present Tense (Plural):** *Wij kauw**en*** (We chew) — The suffix is added.
*   **Past Tense (Weak Verb):** *Ik kauw**de*** (I chewed) — The verb is a **weak verb** (regular), taking the dental suffix *-d-* for the past tense.
*   **Past Participle:** *G**e**kauw**d*** (Chewed) — Takes the prefix *ge-* and the suffix *-d*.

### 4. Important Linguistic Note: Homonymy
From a lexicographical perspective, there is a homonym in Dutch that shares the root `kauw-` but has a different etymology:

*   **`de kauw` (Noun):** Meaning "the crow" (specifically the jackdaw).
    *   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*kawwō**.
    *   **Relationship:** While the verb *kauwen* (chew) and the noun *de kauw* (crow) look identical in the singular stem, they are historically distinct roots. The bird is likely named for its call, whereas the verb is named for the action. However, folk etymology sometimes links them (crows chewing food), but linguistically they are separate entries.
