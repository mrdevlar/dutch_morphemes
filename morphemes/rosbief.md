From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`rosbief`** is a fascinating example of a **lexicalized loan compound**. While it functions as a single word in modern Dutch, its internal structure reveals its English origins.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Breakdown
In terms of segmentation, the word can be divided into two parts:

> **ros** + **bief**

However, from a strictly *Dutch* morphological standpoint, these are not productive morphemes. They are **fossilized roots** (or *cranberry morphemes*) that only exist within this specific lexical item and a few related culinary terms.

### 2. Etymological Origin
The word is a direct loan translation (calque) from the English phrase **"roast beef."**

*   **ros** $\leftarrow$ English **roast**
*   **bief** $\leftarrow$ English **beef**

### 3. Linguistic Analysis of the Components

#### A. The First Morpheme: `ros`
*   **Source:** English *roast* (verb/noun).
*   **Phonological Adaptation:**
    *   **Vowel Shift:** The English diphthong /oʊ/ (as in *roast*) was monophthongized to the Dutch open /ɔ/ (spelled `o`).
    *   **Consonant Deletion:** The final /t/ of *roast* was dropped. This is common in Dutch loanwords where the final consonant is perceived as difficult or unnecessary for the stem (similar to how *film* is used, though *film* keeps the 'm').
*   **Morphological Status:** In Dutch, `ros` is **not a free morpheme**. You cannot use `ros` to mean "roasted" in other contexts (e.g., you do not say *rosde aardappel* for roasted potato; you say *gebraden aardappel*). It is a bound root specific to this loanword.

#### B. The Second Morpheme: `bief`
*   **Source:** English *beef* (noun).
*   **Phonological Adaptation:**
    *   **Vowel Shift:** The English long vowel /iː/ (spelled `ee`) was adapted to the Dutch long vowel /iː/, which is conventionally spelled `ie` in Dutch orthography (e.g., *bief*, *dief*, *brief*).
    *   **Consonant Retention:** The final /f/ was retained.
*   **Morphological Status:** `bief` is a **semi-productive bound morpheme**. While it does not mean "meat" generally (the Dutch word is *rundvlees*), it appears in a small family of culinary loanwords derived from English or French culinary traditions:
    *   *Rosbief* (Roast beef)
    *   *Biefstuk* (Steak, from *beef* + *stuk*)
    *   *Biefstukken* (Plural)
    *   *Biefstuk* is often analyzed as `bief` + `stuk` (piece).

### 4. Semantic and Syntactic Status
*   **Lexicalization:** Although historically a compound of two English words, `rosbief` is now a **single lexical unit** in Dutch. It is treated as a singular noun (*de rosbief*).
*   **Semantic Narrowing:** In English, "roast beef" can refer to the meat generally or the specific dish. In Dutch, `rosbief` almost exclusively refers to the **prepared dish** (slices of cooked beef), whereas the raw meat is referred to as *rundvlees*.
*   **Pluralization:** It follows standard Dutch pluralization rules: *rosbief* $\rightarrow$ *rosbief**en***.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`rosbief`** is a **loan compound**. While it looks like a Dutch compound word, its components (`ros` and `bief`) are not native Dutch morphemes. They are etymological remnants of English that have been phonologically adapted to Dutch spelling conventions and lexicalized into a single noun referring to a specific culinary dish.
