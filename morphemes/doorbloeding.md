From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`doorbloeding`** is a complex noun formed through compounding and derivation. It is a medical and general term meaning "perfusion," "blood supply," or "circulation."

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of the morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

> **`door`** + **`bloed`** + **`-ing`**

Alternatively, from a derivational hierarchy perspective, it is often analyzed as a verb stem plus a suffix:
> **`[door-bloed]`** (Verb Stem) + **`-ing`** (Suffix)

However, the most granular morphological analysis breaks the verb stem down further.

---

### 2. Morpheme-by-Morpheme Analysis

#### **A. `door` (Prefix / Preposition)**
*   **Category:** Preposition functioning as a verbal prefix.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Through," "across," or "by means of."
*   **Function:** In this context, it acts as a **separable verbal prefix** (though fused in the noun form). It modifies the action of the root to indicate that the action penetrates or passes through an object.
*   **Etymology:** Cognate with English *through* and German *durch*.
*   **Linguistic Note:** In Dutch grammar, *door* is often used to create verbs that imply permeation (e.g., *doorlopen* = to walk through, *doorzien* = to see through).

#### **B. `bloed` (Root / Noun Stem)**
*   **Category:** Noun (used here as the semantic root of a verb).
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Blood."
*   **Function:** This is the semantic core of the word. It specifies *what* is flowing. In the verb *doorbloeden*, the noun *bloed* is incorporated into the verbal structure.
*   **Etymology:** Cognate with English *blood* and German *Blut*.
*   **Phonology:** The final consonant is a voiced alveolar stop /d/.

#### **C. `-ing` (Derivational Suffix)**
*   **Category:** Nominalizing suffix.
*   **Function:** This suffix converts the verb *doorbloeden* (to perfuse) into a noun *doorbloeding* (perfusion). It indicates the **process**, **result**, or **state** of the action.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Germanic suffix *-ingaz*, cognate with the English *-ing* (as in *building* or *flowing*).
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, nouns ending in *-ing* are almost exclusively feminine (though grammatically they take the article *de*).
*   **Spelling:** No linking consonant (like *-s-* or *-en-*) is required between *bloed* and *-ing* because the stem ends in a consonant and the suffix begins with a vowel.

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning is built)

The meaning of the word is compositional, meaning the sum of the parts equals the whole:

1.  **`bloed`** (Blood) + **`-en`** (Infinitive marker, implied in the verb stem) = *To blood* (archaic/abstract concept).
2.  **`door`** (Through) + **`bloed`** (Blood) = *To blood through* (The verb **`doorbloeden`**: to supply with blood, to perfuse).
3.  **`doorbloed`** (Verb Stem) + **`-ing`** (Process) = **`doorbloeding`** (The process of blood flowing through).

**Literal Translation:** "Through-blood-ing."
**Actual Meaning:** Perfusion / Blood supply.

---

### 4. Linguistic Notes

*   **Stress Pattern:** In Dutch compounds, the primary stress usually falls on the first element of the compound. However, with the suffix *-ing*, the stress typically falls on the syllable immediately preceding the suffix.
    *   Pronunciation: /ˈdoːrˌbluː.dɪŋ/
    *   Stress: **door**-**BLOE**-ding (Secondary stress on *door*, Primary stress on *bloed*).
*   **Verb Type:** The base verb *doorbloeden* is a **separable verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).
    *   *Infinitive:* *doorbloeden*
    *   *Main Clause:* "Het hart **bloedt** de organen **door**." (The heart perfuses the organs through.)
    *   *Noun Form:* When nominalized with *-ing*, the separable prefix fuses with the root, creating a single written word.
*   **Voicing:** The final *d* in *bloed* remains voiced. If the suffix started with a voiceless consonant, the *d* might devoice (a process called final-obstruent devoicing in Dutch), but since *-ing* starts with a vowel, the *d* remains /d/.

### 5. Comparison to English
While English uses the Latinate term **perfusion** (from Latin *per-* "through" + *fundere* "to pour"), Dutch uses a native Germanic construction.
*   **Dutch:** `door` (through) + `bloed` (blood) + `ing` (process)
*   **English:** `per` (through) + `fus` (pour) + `ion` (process)

Both words share the exact same semantic logic (flowing through), but Dutch builds it transparently from native roots, whereas English uses a loanword.
