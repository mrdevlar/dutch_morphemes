From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`elders`** (meaning "elsewhere") is a fascinating example of a **fossilized** or **lexicalized** form. While modern speakers perceive it as a single, indivisible unit, its internal structure reveals historical morphology.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes in **`elders`**.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into two primary morphemes:

> **`eld-`** + **`-ers`**

*   **`eld-`**: The lexical root (bound root).
*   **`-ers`**: The derivational suffix (adverbial/locative).

---

### 2. Analysis of the Root: `eld-`
*   **Meaning:** "Other" or "different."
*   **Status:** **Bound Root.** In modern Dutch, `eld-` does not exist as an independent word. You cannot say *"Ik ben eld"* to mean "I am other." It only appears in this specific fossilized form.
*   **Etymological Origin:** This root is an **allomorph** (a variant form) of the word **`ander`** (other).
    *   Historically, this stems from the Proto-Germanic **`*alþizō`** (other).
    *   In Old Dutch, the word for "other" was *ander*, but in certain grammatical contexts (specifically the genitive case used for location), the stem shifted.
    *   The shift from *n* to *l* (as in *ander* $\rightarrow$ *eld*) is a result of historical phonological assimilation and dialectal variation common in West Germanic languages.
*   **Linguistic Note:** This is a case of **suppletion** or **allomorphy**. Just as English uses *go/went* (suppletion) or *electric/electricity* (allomorphy), Dutch uses *ander* for the adjective/noun and *eld-* for this specific adverbial form.

### 3. Analysis of the Suffix: `-ers`
*   **Function:** **Locative Adverbializer.** It turns the concept of "other" into a place ("at another place").
*   **Origin:** This suffix is a fusion of two historical elements:
    1.  **`-er-`**: A stem extension often found in Germanic adverbs of place.
    2.  **`-s`**: The **Genitive Case** marker.
*   **Historical Grammar:** In Old Germanic languages, the genitive case was frequently used to denote location (e.g., "at the house" $\rightarrow$ "of the house").
    *   The form *elders* is historically the **genitive singular** of the root *eld*.
    *   Over time, the case system eroded, but the *-s* ending remained as a marker for adverbs of place.
*   **Comparison:** This suffix pattern is visible in other Dutch adverbs of place:
    *   **`ergens`** (somewhere): *erg-* (place) + *-ens* (variant of *-ers*).
    *   **`nergens`** (nowhere): *n-* (negation) + *ergens*.
    *   **`anders`** (differently/other): *ander-* + *-s*.

### 4. Synchronic vs. Diachronic Perspective
To fully understand the word linguistically, one must distinguish between how it is processed today versus how it was formed:

*   **Diachronic (Historical):** The word is transparently **`eld`** (other) + **`-ers`** (genitive/locative). It literally meant "of another place."
*   **Synchronic (Modern):** The word is **opaque**. Modern Dutch speakers do not actively parse `elders` into `eld` + `ers`. It is stored in the mental lexicon as a single atomic unit (a **monomorphemic** word in practice, though **polymorphemic** in history).

### 5. Cognates and Comparative Linguistics
Looking at related languages confirms the morphological analysis:

*   **English:** **`Else`** (as in "someone else").
    *   Etymology: Old English *elles* (genitive of *ell* "other").
    *   Structure: *ell-* (root) + *-es* (genitive suffix).
    *   *Note:* English dropped the *-r-* found in Dutch *elders*, but the structure is identical.
*   **German:** **`anderswo`** (elsewhere).
    *   Structure: *ander-* (other) + *s* (genitive) + *wo* (where).
    *   German retains the *ander-* root more clearly than Dutch *elders*, but uses a compound structure rather than a suffix.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`elders`** is a **fossilized genitive form**. It preserves an ancient grammatical structure (the genitive case indicating location) that has disappeared from the rest of the Dutch language. The root `eld-` is a phonological variant of `ander`, and the suffix `-ers` marks the word as an adverb of place, cognate with the English suffix in "else."
