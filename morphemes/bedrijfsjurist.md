From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`bedrijfsjurist`** is a **compound noun** (*samenstelling*). It is formed by combining two independent nouns with a linking element in between.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Segmentation
The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

> **bedrijf** + **-s-** + **jurist**

---

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. `bedrijf` (Root 1 / Modifier)
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Business," "company," "enterprise," or "undertaking."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *bedrieff*, related to the verb *drijven* ("to drive" or "to conduct"). Historically, it referred to the act of driving or conducting an activity, which evolved to mean the activity of business itself.
*   **Function in Compound:** This is the **modifier** (*bepalend deel*). It specifies the *type* or *domain* of the head noun.

#### B. `-s-` (Linking Element / Interfix)
*   **Type:** Bound morpheme (cannot stand alone).
*   **Linguistic Term:** Interfix, linking element, or *voegklank* (Dutch linguistics).
*   **Function:** It connects the two nouns. It does not carry independent lexical meaning (it is not a plural marker in this context, nor a possessive suffix in the modern sense, though it historically derives from the genitive case).
*   **Phonological Role:** It aids in the phonological flow between the two words, preventing a clash of consonants or vowels, though in this specific case (`bedrijf` ends in a consonant), it is largely morphological.
*   **Historical Context:** In older Dutch (and still in some modern compounds), the `-s-` represented a genitive relationship (similar to English *'s*). Thus, `bedrijfsjurist` historically implies "the lawyer **of** the business."

#### C. `jurist` (Root 2 / Head)
*   **Type:** Free morpheme.
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Lawyer," "legal expert," or "jurist."
*   **Etymology:** Borrowed from Latin *jurista* (one who studies law), which comes from *jus* or *juris* ("law"). It entered Dutch via French (*juriste*) and German (*Jurist*).
*   **Function in Compound:** This is the **head** (*hoofd*). In Dutch compounds, the semantic center is almost always the final element. Therefore, a `bedrijfsjurist` is a specific *type* of `jurist`.

---

### 3. Syntactic and Semantic Structure

*   **Compound Type:** **Endocentric Compound**.
    *   The meaning of the whole word is a hyponym (a specific subtype) of the head word. A `bedrijfsjurist` is a `jurist`.
*   **Semantic Relationship:** **Possessive/Attributive**.
    *   The relationship between the modifier and the head is that of association or possession.
    *   *Interpretation:* "A jurist [who works for/with] a business."
*   **Word Order:** Dutch is a **head-final** language regarding compounds. The specific descriptor comes first (`bedrijf`), and the general category comes last (`jurist`).
    *   *Contrast with English:* English often uses an adjective or a noun adjunct in the same position ("Corporate lawyer"), but the structure is similar.

### 5. Comparison to English
To visualize the morphology:
*   **Dutch:** `bedrijf` (business) + `s` (link) + `jurist` (lawyer)
*   **English:** `corporate` (adj) + `lawyer` (noun) OR `company` (noun) + `'s` (genitive) + `lawyer` (noun)

The Dutch `-s-` functions similarly to the English genitive *'s* in "company's lawyer," but in modern Dutch morphology, it is treated as a fixed linking element within the compound word rather than a grammatical case marker.
