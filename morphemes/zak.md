From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`zak`** is a **monomorphemic** word. This means it consists of only **one morpheme**.

Unlike complex words (e.g., *zakdoek* "handkerchief" = *zak* + *doek*), `zak` cannot be broken down into smaller meaningful units. It is a **free root morpheme**.

Here is the detailed morphological and etymological breakdown:

### 1. Morphological Structure

| Segment | Morpheme Type | Function | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **zak** | **Free Root** | Lexical Noun | Bag, sack, pocket |

*   **Free Morpheme:** It can stand alone as an independent word.
*   **Lexical Morpheme:** It carries the core semantic content (unlike grammatical morphemes like *de* or *en*).
*   **Indivisible:** There are no prefixes (e.g., *be-*, *ge-*) or suffixes (e.g., *-je*, *-en*) attached to this specific form.

### 2. Morphophonology (Pluralization)
While the singular form `zak` is monomorphemic, its plural form demonstrates how this root interacts with Dutch morphological rules:

*   **Singular:** `zak` (/zɑk/)
*   **Plural:** `zakken` (/ˈzɑkə(n)/)

**Analysis:**
To form the plural, the suffix **-en** is added. However, because the root vowel /ɑ/ is short and the root ends in a single consonant, Dutch orthography and phonology require **consonant gemination** (doubling the 'k').
*   *Root:* `zak`
*   *Suffix:* `-en`
*   *Result:* `zak` + `k` + `en` = `zakken`

This doubling preserves the short vowel sound of the root. If it were spelled *zaken*, the vowel would be pronounced long (/zaːkən/), which would change the word's identity (and actually, *zaken* exists as the plural of *zaak* "matter/business").

### 3. Etymological Morphology
To understand the "history" of this morpheme, we look at its cognates and origin:

*   **Modern Dutch:** `zak`
*   **Middle Dutch:** `sac` / `zak`
*   **Old Low Franconian:** `sak`
*   **Proto-Germanic:** **\*sakaz**
*   **Cognates:**
    *   English: **sack**
    *   German: **Sack**
    *   Swedish: **säck**
*   **Ultimate Origin:** Likely borrowed from Latin **saccus** (via Greek *sakkos*), which originally referred to a coarse cloth or sack made from goat hair. Some linguists also link it to the Proto-Indo-European root **\*sek-** ("to cut"), implying a bag made from a cut piece of material.

### 4. Semantic Derivatives (Word Family)
Although `zak` is a single morpheme, it is highly productive. It serves as the base for many compound words and derived verbs.

*   **Compounds (Zak + X):**
    *   `zakdoek` (handkerchief) = *zak* + *doek* (cloth)
    *   `portemonnee` (wallet) = *porte* (carry) + *monnee* (money) — *Note: This is French, but often associated with the concept of a 'zak'.*
    *   `schroefzak` (screw jack) = *schroef* + *zak*
*   **Derivations (Verb forms):**
    *   `zakken` (to drop / to fail an exam) = *zak* + *-en* (verbalizer).
    *   `inzakken` (to sink / to slump) = *in* (prefix) + *zak* + *-en*.

### Summary
From a strict morphological standpoint, **`zak`** is a **single, indivisible lexical root**. It carries the meaning of a container or pocket and serves as the foundational building block for a larger family of Dutch words through compounding and derivation.
