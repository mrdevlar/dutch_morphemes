From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`doelpunt`** is a classic example of a **compound noun**. It consists of two free morphemes joined together to create a new lexical unit with a specific meaning.

Here is the morphological breakdown and analysis:

### 1. Segmentation
The word can be segmented into two distinct morphemes:

> **doel** + **punt**

There are no bound morphemes (affixes) within the base form of the word itself; both components are independent words in the Dutch language.

---

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### Morpheme A: `doel`
*   **Form:** *doel*
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun (neuter gender: *het doel*)
*   **Literal Meaning:** Aim, target, purpose, or goal.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *dōl*, originating from Old Dutch. It is related to the verb *doelen* (to aim). It shares a Proto-Germanic root with the concept of a target.
*   **Function in Compound:** It acts as the **modifier** (or dependent). It specifies *what kind* of point is being discussed.

#### Morpheme B: `punt`
*   **Form:** *punt*
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun (neuter gender: *het punt*)
*   **Literal Meaning:** Point, dot, or spot.
*   **Etymology:** Borrowed from Latin *punctum* (the past participle of *pungere*, meaning "to prick" or "to pierce"). It entered the Germanic languages via French or direct Latin influence during the Middle Ages.
*   **Function in Compound:** It acts as the **head** (or head noun). In Dutch compounds, the semantic head is almost always the final element. Therefore, a *doelpunt* is a type of *punt* (point).

---

### 3. Compound Structure & Semantics

*   **Type:** Endocentric Compound.
    *   This means the compound belongs to the same semantic category as its head. A *doelpunt* is a *punt*.
*   **Semantic Relationship:**
    *   The relationship is **locative** or **associative**. It implies "a point [scored at] the goal" or "the point [associated with] the target."
    *   In sports contexts (football/soccer, hockey, etc.), it specifically refers to the act of scoring or the unit of score awarded for hitting the target.
*   **Orthography:**
    *   In Dutch, compound nouns are typically written as a single closed word (unlike English, which might write "goal point" or "goal-point").
*   **Linking Element (Tussenklank):**
    *   There is **no linking element** (such as *-s-* or *-en-*) between the two morphemes. This is common when the first noun is singular and does not require a genitive or plural marker to connect to the second noun.

---

### 4. Phonological Feature: Stress
In Dutch morphology, compound words typically carry the **primary stress on the first element**.
*   **Pronunciation:** **DOEL**punt (not doelPUNT).
*   This stress pattern helps listeners identify the word as a compound rather than a phrase.

### 5. Inflection (Pluralization)
When the word is pluralized, the inflectional suffix attaches to the **head** of the compound (the second element), not the modifier.
*   **Singular:** *doelpunt*
*   **Plural:** *doelpunten*
    *   *doel* (unchanged) + *punt* + *-en* (plural suffix).

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **punt** | Free Morpheme | Point | Head (Semantic Core) |

**Conclusion:** Linguistically, *doelpunt* is a **neuter compound noun** formed by juxtaposing two free morphemes, where the first specifies the context of the second, with stress falling on the first syllable.
