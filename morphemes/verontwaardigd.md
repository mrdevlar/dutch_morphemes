From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`verontwaardigd`** is a complex morphological structure. It is the past participle of the verb *verontwaardigen* ("to outrage" or "to make indignant"), which functions primarily as an adjective meaning "indignant," "outraged," or "offended."

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation

We can segment the word into five distinct morphemes:

> **`ver-`** + **`ont-`** + **`waard`** + **`-ig`** + **`-d`**

### 2. Morpheme-by-Morpheme Analysis

#### **1. `ver-` (Prefix)**
*   **Type:** Inseparable verbal prefix.
*   **Function:** This prefix often indicates a **change of state**, a **process**, or a **causative** action. It implies that the subject is being subjected to a transformation.
*   **Etymology:** From Proto-Germanic *\*far-*, related to English *for-* (as in *forbid*) and German *ver-*.
*   **In this context:** It turns the concept of "unworthiness" into an active process or a resulting state of being.

#### **2. `ont-` (Prefix)**
*   **Type:** Inseparable verbal prefix.
*   **Function:** This is a **privative** or **reversative** prefix. It indicates removal, separation, or negation (similar to English *un-* or *de-*).
*   **Etymology:** From Proto-Germanic *\*unda-*, related to English *under* and German *ent-*.
*   **In this context:** It negates the root meaning. If *waardig* means "worthy," *ont-* implies "unworthy" or "deprived of worth."

#### **3. `waard` (Root)**
*   **Type:** Lexical Root (Nominal).
*   **Meaning:** "Worth," "value," or "deserving."
*   **Etymology:** From Proto-Germanic *\*wērdaz*.
*   **Cognates:** English **worth**, German *Wert*, Old English *weorð*.
*   **In this context:** This is the semantic core of the word. It refers to a person's dignity, value, or moral standing.

#### **4. `-ig` (Suffix)**
*   **Type:** Derivational Suffix.
*   **Function:** Adjective-forming suffix. It turns a noun or root into an adjective meaning "having the quality of" or "full of."
*   **Etymology:** From Proto-Germanic *\*-agaz*.
*   **Cognates:** English **-y** (as in *worthy*), German *-ig*.
*   **In this context:** It attaches to *waard* to form the adjective *waardig* ("worthy").

#### **5. `-d` (Suffix)**
*   **Type:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** Past participle marker for weak verbs.
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, the past participle of weak verbs typically ends in *-d* or *-t* (depending on the "taco" rule regarding the final sound of the stem).
*   **In this context:** It transforms the verb *verontwaardigen* into the participle *verontwaardigd*, which functions as an adjective describing a state of being.

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning builds)

To understand the full meaning, we must look at how these morphemes layer upon one another historically and logically:

1.  **`waard`** = Worth / Value.
2.  **`waard` + `-ig`** = **`waardig`** (Worthy / Deserving of respect).
3.  **`ont-` + `waardig`** = **`ontwaardig`** (Unworthy / Dishonorable).
    *   *Note:* The verb *ontwaardigen* means "to dishonor" (to strip someone of their worth).
4.  **`ver-` + `ontwaardig`** = **`verontwaardig`** (To cause to be dishonored).
    *   The prefix *ver-* adds a causative or intensive layer. It implies the action of making someone feel their worth has been attacked.
5.  **`verontwaardig` + `-d`** = **`verontwaardigd`** (Having been caused to feel dishonored).
    *   This results in the feeling of **indignation**.

### 4. Linguistic Notes & Comparisons

**Prefix Stacking:**
Dutch allows for the stacking of inseparable prefixes. In this word, *ver-* is the outer prefix and *ont-* is the inner prefix. This is a specific lexicalized combination; you generally cannot swap them (*ontverwaardigd* does not exist).

**The "Indignant" Parallel:**
There is a fascinating cross-linguistic parallel between this Dutch word and the English word **indignant**.
*   **English:** *Indignant* comes from Latin *indignari* (*in-* "not" + *dignus* "worthy"). Literally: "to deem unworthy."
*   **Dutch:** *Verontwaardigd* comes from Germanic roots (*ont-* "not" + *waard* "worthy"). Literally: "made unworthy."
Both words share the exact same semantic logic: **Indignation is the feeling that one's worth has been violated.**

**Grammatical Status:**
While *verontwaardigd* is morphologically a past participle, in modern Dutch usage, it is lexicalized as a pure adjective. You can say *hij is verontwaardigd* (he is indignant), and it does not necessarily imply a passive voice construction (i.e., "he was outraged by someone"), though that meaning is often implied contextually.
