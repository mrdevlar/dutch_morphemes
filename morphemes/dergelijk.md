From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`dergelijk`** is a morphological compound consisting of two distinct morphemes. It is a fossilized phrase that has evolved into a single lexical item.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes:

### 1. Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:
> **der** + **gelijk**

---

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### Morpheme A: `der`
*   **Category:** Demonstrative Pronoun (fossilized Genitive/Dative form).
*   **Etymology:** This form originates from the Old Dutch and Middle Dutch demonstrative pronoun *die* (that). In the historical case system of Dutch, **`der`** was the genitive singular (and dative plural) form of *die*.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Of that" or "from that."
*   **Function:** In modern Dutch, the case system has largely collapsed, and `der` is no longer a productive grammatical ending. However, in this compound, it retains its historical function as a **deictic marker**. It points back to a previously mentioned noun or situation.
*   **Cognates:** It is cognate with the German genitive article *der* (as in *derer*) and the English demonstrative *the* (which shares the same Proto-Germanic root *þiz-*, though the specific form *der* is closer to German).

#### Morpheme B: `gelijk`
*   **Category:** Adjective / Adverb.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *gelīk*, from Proto-Germanic *\*galīkaz*.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Equal," "same," "like," or "similar."
*   **Function:** This is the semantic head of the compound. It establishes the relationship of similarity or equivalence.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   **English:** *Like* (Note: English *like* and Dutch *gelijk* are cognates, though they diverged in usage).
    *   **German:** *Gleich* (equal/same).
    *   **Frisian:** *Lik*.

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (How they combine)

When combined, the morphemes create a specific semantic meaning through **compounding**:

*   **Literal Construction:** "Of-that-equal" or "That-similar."
*   **Idiomatic Meaning:** "Such," "similar to that," or "of that kind."

The logic follows a possessive or partitive structure common in older Germanic languages: *Something that is equal **of that** [previously mentioned thing]*.

**Example:**
*   *Ik heb **dergelijk** geld nodig.* (I need **such** money.)
*   *Dit is **dergelijk** gedrag.* (This is **such** behavior.)

---

### 4. Morphosyntactic Features

While `dergelijk` is often treated as a single word, it retains some morphological flexibility typical of Dutch adjectives:

1.  **Inflection:** It often takes the adjectival inflection suffix **-e** when used attributively before a noun (unless the noun is neuter singular indefinite).
    *   *Dergelijke* mensen (Such people)
    *   *Dergelijk* gedrag (Such behavior - neuter singular)
2.  **Nominalization:** It can be nominalized with the suffix **-s** to mean "such things" or "the like."
    *   *Dergelijks* (Such things / The like).
3.  **Orthography:** Historically, this was written as two words (*der gelijcke*). Modern Dutch orthography fuses them into one word (*dergelijk*), marking its status as a lexicalized compound.

### 5. Cross-Linguistic Comparison

To understand the structure better, it helps to compare it to English and German:

*   **Dutch:** *der* (of that) + *gelijk* (like) = **Dergelijk**
*   **German:** *der* (of that) + *gleichen* (like/same) = **Dergleichen**
*   **English:** *such* (Old English *swylc* = *swā* "so" + *ylc* "like")

While English uses a fused word (*such*), Dutch and German preserve the transparent morphological structure of "demonstrative + like."

### Summary
*   **der**: Fossilized genitive demonstrative ("of that").
*   **gelijk**: Adjective meaning "equal/like."
*   **dergelijk**: A compound adjective meaning "of that kind" or "such."
