From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`gouden`** (meaning "golden") is a clear example of **derivational morphology**. It is formed by combining a lexical root with a suffix to change the part of speech from a noun to an adjective.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation

The word can be segmented into two morphemes:

> **`goud`** + **`-en`**

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `goud`
*   **Form:** Free morpheme (can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Gold" (the chemical element or the metal).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *goud*, from Proto-Germanic **\*gulþą**. It is cognate with English *gold* and German *Gold*.
*   **Phonology:** In Standard Dutch, this is pronounced /ɣʌut/ (with a voiced velar fricative /ɣ/ and a diphthong /ʌut/).

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Form:** Bound morpheme (cannot stand alone).
*   **Type:** **Derivational Suffix**.
*   **Function:** It converts the noun *goud* into an adjective. It denotes "made of," "consisting of," or "having the quality of" the root noun.
*   **Etymology:** This suffix originates from the Proto-Germanic adjectival suffix **\*-ina-**. In Old Dutch, this appeared as *-in* or *-en*.
*   **Cognates:** This is directly cognate with the English adjectival suffix *-en* (as in *wooden*, *golden*, *woolen*) and the German *-en* (as in *golden*).

### 3. Grammatical Function and Usage

In Modern Dutch, the relationship between the root and the suffix is **lexicalized**. This means that *gouden* is treated as the base form of the adjective, rather than *goud* being an adjective that simply takes an ending.

*   **Noun:** *Het **goud*** (The gold).
*   **Adjective:** *Een **gouden** ring* (A golden ring).

Unlike many other Dutch adjectives (e.g., *rood* $\rightarrow$ *rode*), the adjective *gouden* does not typically drop the *-en* in attributive position.
*   *Correct:* *De **gouden** sleutel* (The golden key).
*   *Incorrect:* *De goud sleutel*.

Therefore, the *-en* is not an inflectional ending (like a plural marker or a verb conjugation); it is an integral part of the adjective's derivation.

### 4. Historical Linguistic Context

From a diachronic (historical) perspective, this word illustrates a common West Germanic pattern:

1.  **Proto-Germanic:** **\*gulþaz** (noun) + **\*-ina-** (adjectival suffix).
2.  **Old Dutch:** *goud* + *-in*.
3.  **Middle Dutch:** *goud* + *-en* (due to vowel shifts and final syllable reduction).
4.  **Modern Dutch:** *gouden*.

### Summary Table

| Morpheme | Type | Meaning | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`goud`** | Root (Free) | Gold | Denotes the material/substance. |
| **`-en`** | Suffix (Bound) | Made of / Possessing | Derives an adjective from the noun. |
| **`gouden`** | **Word** | **Golden** | **Adjective describing quality/material.** |

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`gouden`** is a **denominal adjective**. The morpheme **`-en`** is a derivational suffix inherited from Proto-Germanic that transforms the noun **`goud`** into an adjective, signifying possession of the qualities of gold. It is structurally identical to the English word *golden*.
