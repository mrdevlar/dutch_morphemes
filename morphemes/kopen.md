From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`kopen`** (meaning "to buy") can be analyzed as a verb in its **infinitive form**.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of its components.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of **two morphemes**:

> **`koop`** + **`-en`**

### 2. Detailed Explanation of Morphemes

#### A. The Root/Stem: `koop-`
*   **Type:** Lexical Root (Free/Bound depending on context).
*   **Function:** This morpheme carries the core semantic meaning of the word: "buy."
*   **Status:** In the infinitive form (`kopen`), this root is bound to the suffix. However, in the imperative mood (e.g., *"Koop dat!"* / "Buy that!"), `koop` functions as a **free morpheme** because it can stand alone as a command.
*   **Phonology:** The vowel `oo` represents a long close-mid back rounded vowel (/oː/).
*   **Allomorphy:** It is important to note that this root is not entirely stable across all tenses. While the infinitive and present tense use the long `oo` (*ik koop*), the past tense changes to a short vowel with a consonant change (*ik kocht*). This indicates that `kopen` is historically a **strong verb** (showing ablaut/vowel gradation), even though it conjugates like a weak verb in the present tense.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** This morpheme marks the **infinitive** form of the verb. In Dutch grammar, the infinitive is the citation form (the form you find in a dictionary).
*   **Grammar:** It does not change the meaning of the root; it only indicates the grammatical category (non-finite verb).
*   **Phonology:** In standard pronunciation, this is often realized as a schwa followed by a nasal consonant (/ə(n)/). In rapid speech, the /n/ is sometimes dropped (e.g., *kopen* sounds like *koope*).

### 3. Etymological Context (Historical Linguistics)
To fully understand the morphology, one must look at the word's origin, which explains why it behaves slightly differently from native Germanic verbs.

*   **Origin:** `Kopen` is a **loanword**. It entered the Dutch language via Old High German *kōfan*, which was borrowed from the Latin verb *cōmperāre* (to buy/procure).
*   **Linguistic Impact:** Because it is a loanword, it does not follow the strict Proto-Germanic ablaut patterns of native strong verbs (like *singen - zong - gezongen*). However, it retained a vestigial strong past tense (*kocht*) rather than the regular weak past tense (*koopte*), which would be expected for a modern weak verb.
*   **Morphological Classification:** Despite the irregular past tense, in the specific form `kopen`, it follows the standard **Class 1 Weak Verb** pattern of the infinitive (Stem + `-en`).

### 5. Comparison with Related Forms
To see how the morphemes interact in different contexts:

*   **Infinitive:** `koop` + `en` = **kopen** (to buy)
*   **Present (1st Person):** `koop` + `∅` (zero suffix) = **koop** (I buy)
*   **Present (3rd Person):** `koop` + `t` = **koopt** (he/she buys)
*   **Past:** `koch` (root change) + `t` = **kocht** (bought)
*   **Participle:** `ge` + `koch` + `t` = **gekocht** (bought)

### Conclusion
In the specific form **`kopen`**, the word is **bimorphemic**. It is composed of the lexical root **`koop`** and the inflectional infinitive suffix **`-en`**. While the root shows historical irregularity in other tenses, in this specific form, it adheres to the standard Dutch morphological structure for infinitives.
