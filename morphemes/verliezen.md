From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch verb **`verliezen`** (to lose) can be analyzed by breaking it down into its constituent morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown, followed by a detailed explanation of each part.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word is segmented as follows:

> **ver-** + **lies** + **-en**

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. Prefix: `ver-`
*   **Type:** Derivational Prefix.
*   **Function:** This prefix modifies the meaning of the root verb. In Dutch, *ver-* is highly productive and often indicates a change of state, a process of completion, or a negative/away direction.
*   **Semantics in this context:** Here, it adds a sense of "away" or "mis-." It transforms the concept of the root (separating/loosing) into the concept of "losing" something permanently or unintentionally.
*   **Examples:** *veranderen* (to change), *verdrinken* (to drown/lose life in water), *vergeten* (to forget).

#### B. Root: `lies`
*   **Type:** Bound Root (Lexical Root).
*   **Status:** In Modern Dutch, this is a **bound morpheme**. You cannot use *lies* as a standalone verb (you cannot say *"Ik lies"*). It only appears within derived words like *verliezen* (to lose) or the noun *verlies* (loss).
*   **Etymology:** This root comes from the Old Dutch *līsan* and Proto-Germanic *\*līsan*, which meant "to loose," "to untie," or "to separate."
*   **Semantic Shift:** Historically, the meaning shifted from "to untie/separate" to "to be separated from" (i.e., to lose).
*   **Cognates:** This root is etymologically related to the English word **lose** (from Old English *losian*) and the German **verlieren**. It is also related to the English word **loose** (though the spelling diverged).

#### C. Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** This is the standard marker for the **infinitive** form of Dutch verbs.
*   **Grammar:** It indicates that the verb is in its base form, not conjugated for person or tense.
*   **Examples:** *loop**en*** (to walk), *werk**en*** (to work), *zi**en*** (to see).

---

### 3. Grammatical Context: Strong Verb
From a morphological standpoint, it is important to note that *verliezen* is a **strong verb** (irregular verb). This means the root vowel changes to indicate tense, rather than adding a *-d* or *-t* suffix for the past tense.

*   **Infinitive:** ver**lie**zen (Root vowel: *ie* /i:/)
*   **Past Tense (Singular):** ver**loor** (Root vowel: *oo* /o:/)
*   **Past Participle:** ver**loor**en (Root vowel: *oo* /o:/)

This vowel gradation (*ie* $\rightarrow$ *oo* $\rightarrow$ *oo*) is a remnant of the Germanic ablaut system.

### 4. Common Linguistic Pitfall: `lezen` vs. `lies`
A common confusion for learners (and a linguistic false friend) is the similarity between the root **`lies`** and the verb **`lezen`** (to read).

*   **`verliezen`** (to lose): Root *lies* (from *līsan* = to loose/separate).
*   **`lezen`** (to read): Root *lez* (from *lesan* = to gather/select).

While they look similar in the infinitive form (*verliezen* vs. *lezen*), they are **etymologically unrelated**. There is no morpheme *ver-* + *lezen* in Dutch.
