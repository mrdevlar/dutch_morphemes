From a linguistic perspective, it is important to first clarify that **`worstelen met`** is not a single word, but rather a **verb phrase** consisting of an infinitive verb and a preposition.

Below is the morphological and syntactic breakdown of the components.

---

### 1. The Verb: *worstelen*
This word can be broken down into three distinct morphemes. It is a **weak (regular) verb**.

| Morpheme | Type | Function/Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **worst-** | **Root (Free/Bound)** | The core semantic meaning. Historically, this root relates to "struggle" or "wrestle" (cognate with English *wrestle*). <br>*(Note: In modern Dutch, the noun `worst` means "sausage," creating a homonym, but the etymological root here is the struggle.)* |
| **-el-** | **Derivational Suffix** | This is a frequentative or verb-forming suffix. It turns the root into a verb stem indicating an action that is repeated or involves effort. It creates the stem *worstel-*. |
| **-en** | **Inflectional Suffix** | This is the standard infinitive marker for Dutch verbs. It indicates the non-finite form of the verb (equivalent to English "to"). |

**Morphological Structure:**
`[worst]` (Root) + `[el]` (Derivational) + `[en]` (Inflectional)

**Synchronic Note:**
For a modern Dutch speaker, the word is often perceived as having a stem of **`worstel-`** and an ending of **`-en`**, because the root *worst* (in the sense of struggle) is no longer a productive, standalone word in the modern lexicon (unlike the sausage).

---

### 2. The Preposition: *met*
This word is a single, indivisible unit.

| Morpheme | Type | Function/Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **met** | **Free Morpheme** | A preposition. It is **monomorphemic** (cannot be broken down further). |

**Function:**
In this context, *met* indicates the **instrument** or the **counterpart** of the struggle. It translates to "with."

---

### 3. The Phrase: *worstelen met*
From a syntactic and semantic perspective, this combination forms a **Prepositional Verb** (in Dutch: *voorzetselwerkwoord*).

*   **Syntax:** The verb *worstelen* requires the preposition *met* to introduce the object of the struggle. You cannot simply say "Ik worstel de taak" (I struggle the task); you must say "Ik worstel **met** de taak" (I struggle **with** the task).
*   **Semantics:** The combination creates an idiomatic meaning. While *worstelen* literally means "to wrestle," *worstelen met* is used metaphorically to mean "to grapple with," "to struggle with," or "to have difficulty dealing with" (e.g., a problem, a concept, or an emotion).
*   **Word Order:** In a main clause, the verb conjugates and moves to the second position, while the preposition stays with the object.
    *   *Example:* "Ik **worstel** met dit probleem." (I struggle with this problem.)

### Linguistic Trivia: The "Sausage" Confusion
A common point of confusion for learners is the root **`worst`**.
*   **Noun:** *De worst* = The sausage.
*   **Verb Root:** *Worstelen* = To struggle.

These are **homonyms** (words that sound the same but have different origins/meanings). The verb *worstelen* is etymologically linked to the Old High German *wurst* (wrestling/struggle), whereas the sausage *worst* comes from a different root related to "twisting" meat. In modern Dutch morphology, they are treated as separate lexical entries despite the identical spelling of the root.
