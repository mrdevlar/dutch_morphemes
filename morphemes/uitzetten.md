From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`uitzetten`** is a classic example of a **separable compound verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*). It is composed of three distinct morphemes that combine to create a specific semantic meaning that often differs from the literal sum of its parts.

Here is the morphological breakdown and analysis.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:

> **`uit`** + **`zet`** + **`-en`**

*   **`uit`**: Prefix / Separable Particle
*   **`zet`**: Verb Root (Stem)
*   **`-en`**: Infinitive Suffix

---

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. `uit` (Prefix / Particle)
*   **Category:** Preposition / Adverb functioning as a derivational prefix.
*   **Phonology:** /œy̯t/ (Standard Dutch). In the infinitive form `uitzetten`, the primary stress falls on this syllable: **ˈuit**zetten.
*   **Function:**
    *   **Directional:** Literally indicates movement "out" or "away."
    *   **Negation/Completion:** In the context of devices (lights, engines), it functions similarly to an off-switch, implying a state of "out of operation."
    *   **Derivational:** It changes the lexical meaning of the root verb `zetten`.
*   **Syntactic Behavior:** This is a **separable** prefix. In a main clause, `uit` detaches from the verb and moves to the end of the sentence.
    *   *Infinitive:* Ik ga het licht **uitzetten**. (I am going to turn off the light.)
    *   *Main Clause:* Ik **zet** het licht **uit**. (I turn off the light.)

#### B. `zet` (Verb Root)
*   **Category:** Lexical Root (Stem).
*   **Phonology:** /zɛt/.
*   **Meaning:** To place, to set, to put. It implies a deliberate action of positioning something.
*   **Morphological Status:** This is a **bound root** in this context. While `zetten` exists as a standalone verb, the root `zet` cannot stand alone as a finite verb without inflection (like `-en`, `-t`, `-te`, etc.).
*   **Cognates:**
    *   English: **Set**
    *   German: **setzen**
    *   Old English: *settan*

#### C. `-en` (Suffix)
*   **Category:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** Marks the verb as the **infinitive** form (the dictionary form).
*   **Phonology:** /ə/ (schwa) or /n/ depending on dialect and speech rate.
*   **Morphological Status:** This is a **bound morpheme**. It cannot exist independently.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *-an*, related to the English infinitive marker (e.g., *to walk* vs. *walk*, though English has largely lost this suffix).

---

### 3. Semantic Polysemy (Multiple Meanings)
Linguistically, `uitzetten` is a prime example of **polysemy**, where one form has multiple related meanings. The meaning depends heavily on the direct object:

1.  **To Turn Off:** (Most common usage).
    *   *Context:* Electronics, lights, engines.
    *   *Logic:* `uit` (out) + `zetten` (set) = Set to "out" (off state).
    *   *Example:* *De radio uitzetten.* (Turn off the radio.)
2.  **To Set Out / Arrange:**
    *   *Context:* Furniture, goods, traps.
    *   *Logic:* Literal placement.
    *   *Example:* *Meubels uitzetten.* (Set out furniture.)
3.  **To Transplant:**
    *   *Context:* Gardening/Agriculture.
    *   *Logic:* Moving a plant from a pot *out* into the ground.
    *   *Example:* *Planten uitzetten.* (Transplant plants.)
4.  **To Expand / Stretch:**
    *   *Context:* Clothing, fabric.
    *   *Logic:* The material is "set out" wider than before.
    *   *Example:* *De trui is uitgezet.* (The sweater has stretched/shrunk out of shape.)

---

### 4. Morphophonology (The "Ge-" Rule)
A crucial linguistic feature of Dutch separable verbs is how they form the **past participle**.

*   **Rule:** The prefix `uit` is separated from the root `zet`, and the participial marker `ge-` is inserted **between** them.
*   **Formation:** `uit` + **`ge`** + `zet` + `t`
*   **Result:** **`uitgezet`**
*   **Significance:** This insertion proves that `uit` is a separable prefix. If it were an inseparable prefix (like `be-` in `bezitten`), the `ge-` would not appear (e.g., *bezeten*, not *gebezeten*).

### 5. Etymological Summary
*   **`uit`**: From Proto-Germanic *\*ut*, related to English *out* and German *aus*.
*   **`zetten`**: From Proto-Germanic *\*satjaną*, related to English *set* and German *setzen*.
*   **`-en`**: From Proto-Germanic *-an*.

**Linguistic Takeaway:** `Uitzetten` demonstrates the Germanic tendency toward **verb-particle constructions**. Unlike English, where "turn off" uses a distinct verb ("turn") + particle ("off"), Dutch often uses a core verb ("zetten") + particle ("uit") to create a new lexical item with a specific, often idiomatic, meaning.
