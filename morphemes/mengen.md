From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`mengen`** (meaning "to mix" or "to blend") can be analyzed through **morphology**, the study of the internal structure of words.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of its morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two morphemes:

> **meng** + **en**

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root/Stem: `meng-`
*   **Type:** **Free Lexical Morpheme** (Root).
*   **Function:** This is the core semantic unit of the word. It carries the primary meaning ("to mix").
*   **Status:** In this specific context, it functions as the **verb stem**. While it cannot stand alone as a complete grammatical verb in standard Dutch (you cannot say "Ik meng" without a conjugation ending, though in imperative it can be "Meng!"), it is the base to which inflectional endings are attached.
*   **Etymology:** It derives from the Proto-Germanic **\*mangjaną**. It is cognate with the German *mengen* and the English word *mingle* (which shares the same Germanic root).
*   **Phonology:** /mɛŋ/ (The `g` is velar, sounding like the `ng` in English "sing").

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** **Bound Inflectional Morpheme**.
*   **Function:** This is an **infinitive marker**. In Dutch, the suffix `-en` (or sometimes just `-n` after a vowel) is the standard ending used to form the dictionary form (infinitive) of a verb.
*   **Grammatical Category:** It marks the verb as non-finite (not conjugated for person or number).
*   **Note on Homophony:** This specific string of letters (`-en`) serves multiple functions in Dutch morphology. In the word *mengen*, it marks the **infinitive**. However, in a sentence like *wij mengen* ("we mix"), the same `-en` suffix marks the **plural present tense**. In the word *mengen* in isolation, it is strictly the infinitive marker.

### 3. Morphological Classification
*   **Word Formation Process:** **Suffixation** (specifically, inflectional suffixation).
*   **Verb Class:** **Weak Verb** (Regular).
    *   We know this because the past tense is formed by adding a dental suffix (`-d-` or `-t-`) to the stem (*mengde*), rather than changing the vowel of the root (which would make it a strong verb, like *lopen* → *liep*).
*   **Transitivity:** It is a transitive verb (usually requires an object, e.g., *Ik meng de ingrediënten*).
