From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`stresshormoon`** is a classic example of a **closed compound noun**. It is formed through the process of **compounding**, where two independent lexical items are joined to create a new word with a specific meaning.

Here is the morphological breakdown and linguistic explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two **free morphemes** (roots that can stand alone as words):

> **`stress`** + **`hormoon`**

There are no bound morphemes (affixes like prefixes or suffixes) attached to these roots in this specific singular form.

---

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### Morpheme A: `stress`
*   **Type:** Free Root Morpheme (Noun).
*   **Etymology:** This is a **loanword** (Anglicism) borrowed directly from English. In English, it derives from the Old French *estrece* (narrowness, oppression), which evolved to mean "distress" and eventually "pressure/tension."
*   **Function:** In this compound, it acts as the **modifier** (or determiner). It specifies the *type* or *cause* of the hormone.
*   **Semantics:** Refers to the physiological or psychological state of strain.

#### Morpheme B: `hormoon`
*   **Type:** Free Root Morpheme (Noun).
*   **Etymology:** This is an **internationalism** derived from Greek. It comes from the Greek verb *hormōn* (impelling, setting in motion), from *hormē* (impulse). It entered Dutch via Latin and French/English scientific terminology.
*   **Function:** In this compound, it acts as the **head** of the word.
*   **Semantics:** Refers to a chemical messenger produced by glands.
*   **Headedness:** Dutch compounds are **right-headed**. This means the semantic category of the whole word is determined by the rightmost element. Therefore, a `stresshormoon` is a type of `hormoon`, not a type of `stress`.

### 3. Syntactic and Phonological Features

#### The Compound Structure (N + N)
*   **Structure:** Noun + Noun.
*   **Linking Element:** There is **no linking morpheme** (such as *-s-* or *-en-*) between the two roots. In Dutch, linking elements are common (e.g., *liefde**s**verklaring*), but in this specific case, the two nouns are juxtaposed directly.
*   **Orthography:** It is written as a **closed compound** (one word). While hyphenated forms (*stress-hormoon*) exist in informal usage or for clarity, standard Dutch orthography (Van Dale) prefers the closed form.

#### Stress Pattern
*   **Primary Stress:** In Dutch compounds, the primary lexical stress typically falls on the **first constituent**.
*   **Pronunciation:** **`STRESS`**-hormoon.
*   **Linguistic Significance:** This stress pattern distinguishes the compound from a noun phrase. If you said "stress hormoon" (with equal stress or stress on the second word), it would sound like two separate words rather than a single lexical unit.

Linguistically, **`stresshormoon`** is a **right-headed, endocentric compound noun** composed of two loanword roots. It demonstrates the Dutch language's ability to integrate foreign terminology (English and Greek) seamlessly into its native morphological structure without requiring additional linking sounds.
