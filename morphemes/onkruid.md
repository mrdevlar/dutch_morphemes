From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`onkruid`** (meaning "weed") is a fascinating example of derivational morphology and semantic shift.

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes, their functions, and their etymological history.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two morphemes:

> **on-** + **kruid**

*   **on-**: Bound morpheme (Prefix)
*   **kruid**: Free morpheme (Root/Base)

---

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Prefix: `on-`
*   **Type:** Derivational Prefix.
*   **Function:** Negation.
*   **Meaning:** "Not," "un-," or "lack of."
*   **Linguistic Context:**
    *   This is the Dutch cognate of the English prefix **`un-`** (e.g., *unhappy*, *undo*) and the German prefix **`un-`** (e.g., *unmöglich*).
    *   **Etymology:** It stems from the Proto-Germanic **\*un-**, which in turn comes from the Proto-Indo-European **\*n̥-** (a negative particle).
    *   **Usage in Dutch:** While `on-` is most commonly used to negate adjectives (e.g., *onmogelijk* = impossible), it is also used to create nouns that denote the absence or opposite of the root concept (e.g., *onrust* = unrest, *onzin* = nonsense).
    *   **Phonology:** Note that the /n/ in `on-` undergoes **nasal assimilation** before the velar stop /k/ in `kruid`. It is pronounced as a velar nasal /ŋ/ (like the 'ng' in "sing").
        *   *IPA:* /ˈɔŋ.krœy̯t/

#### B. The Root: `kruid`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Noun).
*   **Meaning:** Herb, plant, spice, or greenery.
*   **Linguistic Context:**
    *   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *krut*, Old Dutch *krud*, and Proto-Germanic **\*krudą**.
    *   **Cognates:** It is related to the English word **cress** (though the semantic path diverged significantly). It is *not* related to the English word "grass" (which comes from a different Proto-Germanic root, **\*grasą**).
    *   **Usage:** In modern Dutch, *kruid* usually refers to culinary herbs (*kruiden*) or medicinal plants.

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning is formed)

When combined, the morphemes create a compound/derived noun with a specific semantic logic:

1.  **Literal Construction:** "Not-herb" or "Un-plant."
2.  **Actual Meaning:** "Weed."
3.  **The Semantic Shift:**
    *   Linguistically, this is a case of **pejoration** or **negative valuation**.
    *   A "weed" is botanically a plant. However, in the context of agriculture or gardening, it is a plant that is growing where it is not wanted.
    *   Therefore, the Dutch language conceptualizes a weed not as a specific *type* of plant, but as a **plant that lacks the status of a useful herb**. It is defined by what it is *not* (a useful *kruid*) rather than what it *is*.

### 4. Grammatical Classification
*   **Word Class:** Noun (Zelfstandig naamwoord).
*   **Gender:** Neuter (*Het onkruid*).
*   **Plural:** *Onkruiden* (rarely used) or *onkruid* (often treated as a mass noun, like "grass" or "furniture" in English).

### Conclusion
From a morphological standpoint, **`onkruid`** is a **derived noun** formed by attaching a negative prefix to a noun root. It illustrates how language categorizes objects based on utility: a plant is only a "weed" (*onkruid*) when it fails to meet the criteria of being a "herb" (*kruid*).
