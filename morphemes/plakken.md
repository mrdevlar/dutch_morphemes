From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`plakken`** is an interesting case because it is **homographic** (spelled the same) and **homophonous** (pronounced the same) for two distinct morphological structures: a **verb** (infinitive) and a **noun** (plural).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of both possibilities.

---

### 1. The Root Morpheme: `plak-`
Regardless of whether the word functions as a verb or a noun, the core lexical unit (the root) is the same.

*   **Morpheme:** `plak`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (it can stand alone as a word).
*   **Phonology:** /plɑk/ (The 'a' is a short, open back unrounded vowel).
*   **Semantics:** The core meaning relates to **adhesion**, **flatness**, or a **viscous substance**.
    *   *As a verb stem:* To stick, to glue, to plaster.
    *   *As a noun stem:* A slice, a patch, a blob, or a sticky substance.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *placken* or *plak*. It is likely onomatopoeic in origin, imitating the sound of something flat and sticky hitting a surface.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   German: *Plack* (patch), *placken* (to patch/work hard).
    *   English: *Plaque* (via French, related to the concept of a flat plate/stick).
    *   Frisian: *plak* (place/spot).

---

### 2. The Suffix Morpheme: `-en`
The suffix `-en` is a **bound morpheme**. In Dutch, this specific string of letters serves two different grammatical functions depending on the category of the root.

#### Case A: The Verb (Infinitive)
In the context of the verb *plakken* ("to stick"):
*   **Morpheme:** `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional suffix.
*   **Function:** **Infinitive Marker**. In Dutch, the infinitive form of almost all verbs ends in `-en` (or `-n` if the stem ends in a vowel).
*   **Grammar:** It marks the word as the base form of the verb, used after modal verbs (e.g., *Ik wil plakken* - I want to stick) or as a command.
*   **Conjugation:** It is a **weak (regular) verb**.
    *   Present: *ik plak* (I stick)
    *   Past: *ik plakte* (I stuck)
    *   Participle: *geplakt* (stuck)

#### Case B: The Noun (Plural)
In the context of the noun *plak* ("a slice/patch"):
*   **Morpheme:** `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional suffix.
*   **Function:** **Plural Marker**. This is the most common plural suffix in Dutch (similar to English *-s* or *-es*).
*   **Grammar:** It indicates that there is more than one "slice" or "patch."
    *   Singular: *een plak kaas* (a slice of cheese).
    *   Plural: *twee plakken kaas* (two slices of cheese).
*   **Noun Class:** *De-plak* (Common gender).

### 4. Derivational Potential
The root `plak-` is highly productive in Dutch morphology, meaning it frequently combines with other morphemes to create new words (derivatives). Examples include:

*   **`plak` + `band`** = *Plakband* (Adhesive tape / Duct tape).
*   **`plak` + `ker`** = *Plakker* (Sticker / Glue / One who sticks).
*   **`aan` + `plak` + `en`** = *Aanplakken* (To paste up/post up, e.g., a notice).
*   **`plak` + `s`** = *Plaksel* (Glue/Paste - using the suffix *-sel* to create a noun of substance).

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`plakken`** is a **zero-derived** or **conversion** pair where the same phonological form serves two different parts of speech. The distinction relies entirely on **syntax** (how the word is used in a sentence) rather than morphology (the internal structure of the word itself), as both the verb and the noun plural share the exact same root and suffix combination.
