From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`onderuitgaan`** is a complex verb formed through compounding and the combination of prepositional particles. It is a classic example of a **separable verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of the word.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

> **`onder`** + **`uit`** + **`gaan`**

### 2. Analysis of Individual Morphemes

#### A. `onder`
*   **Category:** Preposition / Adverb
*   **Meaning:** "Under," "below," or "beneath."
*   **Function:** In this compound, it functions as a directional particle indicating a downward trajectory or a position beneath a standard of stability.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *under*, from Proto-Germanic *\*undar*, and ultimately Proto-Indo-European (PIE) *\*ndher-* (under).
*   **Cognates:** English *under*, German *unter*.

#### B. `uit`
*   **Category:** Preposition / Adverb
*   **Meaning:** "Out."
*   **Function:** Indicates direction away from an interior or a source. In this specific combination, it implies a state of being "finished," "exhausted," or "removed" from a viable state.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *ut*, from Proto-Germanic *\*ut*, and PIE *\*ud-* (up, out).
*   **Cognates:** English *out*, German *aus*.

#### C. `gaan`
*   **Category:** Verb (Infinitive)
*   **Meaning:** "To go."
*   **Function:** This is the **lexical head** of the word. It provides the core action.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *gān*, from Proto-Germanic *\*gāną*.
*   **Cognates:** German *gehen*. (Note: English *go* is cognate but suppletive; the Germanic root is preserved in *gone* and *went* historically, though *gaan* aligns more closely with *go* in other Germanic languages).

### 3. Syntactic Structure: The Separable Verb
In Dutch morphology, `onderuitgaan` is classified as a **separable verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).

*   **In the Infinitive:** The morphemes are fused into one word: *onderuitgaan*.
*   **In a Main Clause:** The verb stem (`gaan`) moves to the second position, and the particle complex (`onderuit`) moves to the end of the clause.
    *   *Example:* "De zaak **gaat** morgen **onderuit**." (The business **goes** down tomorrow.)
*   **In the Subordinate Clause:** The verb remains at the end, but the particle and stem fuse again.
    *   *Example:* "...dat de zaak morgen **onderuitgaat**." (...that the business **goes down** tomorrow.)

**Linguistic Note on `onderuit`:**
While `onder` and `uit` are two separate morphemes, in this specific lexical item, they function together as a **complex particle**. They do not modify each other sequentially (i.e., it is not "going out from under" in a literal spatial sense); rather, they form a fixed idiomatic unit that modifies the verb `gaan`.

### 4. Semantic Composition (Meaning)
The meaning of the compound is **idiomatic** rather than purely compositional.

*   **Literal Interpretation:** "To go out from under." (This is rarely used literally).
*   **Idiomatic Meaning:** To decline, to deteriorate, to fail, to crash, or to collapse.
    *   *Contexts:* It is frequently used regarding businesses (*een bedrijf gaat onderuit*), health (*zijn gezondheid gaat onderuit*), or situations (*de situatie gaat onderuit*).
*   **Semantic Shift:** The combination suggests a movement from a position of support (`onder` implies being under something stable) to a state of being "out" (`uit` implies finished or depleted). It metaphorically describes a fall from stability into ruin.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`onderuitgaan`** is a **verb-particle compound** consisting of two directional adverbs (`onder`, `uit`) and a motion verb (`gaan`). It exemplifies the Dutch tendency to create specific semantic nuances by stacking prepositional particles before a verb root, resulting in a separable verb with a distinct idiomatic meaning of "decline" or "failure."
