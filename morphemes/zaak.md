From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`zaak`** is a **monomorphemic** word. This means it consists of only one morpheme.

Here is the detailed morphological and etymological breakdown:

### 1. Morphological Structure
*   **Word:** `zaak`
*   **Morphemes:** 1
*   **Breakdown:** `zaak` (Root)
*   **Classification:** Free Morpheme / Lexical Root

Because `zaak` contains no prefixes (like *be-*, *ge-*, *ver-*) and no suffixes (like *-heid*, *-te*, *-je*), it cannot be segmented further. It is the base form upon which other words are built.

### 2. Etymological Analysis
To understand the morphology fully, we must look at the history of the root, as the meaning has shifted over time.

*   **Old Dutch:** *saca*
*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*sakō*
*   **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** Likely related to *\*segh-* (to hold) or *\*sak-* (to be able/have power), though the exact PIE root is debated.

**Cognates:**
*   **German:** *Sache* (thing, matter)
*   **English:** *sake* (as in "for goodness' sake," originally meaning "for the cause/matter")
*   **Frisian:** *sake*

### 3. Semantic Evolution (Polysemy)
The fact that `zaak` is a single root explains why it carries multiple related meanings in modern Dutch. This is a result of **semantic broadening** and **metonymy**.

1.  **Original Meaning (Legal):** In Old Germanic languages, the root *\*sakō* referred specifically to a "lawsuit," "accusation," or "legal dispute."
2.  **Broadening:** Over time, the meaning expanded from a specific legal dispute to any "matter" or "issue" at hand.
3.  **Metonymy:** From "matter of business," it came to refer to the "business" itself, and eventually the physical location where business is conducted (a "shop" or "store").

**Modern Meanings:**
*   *Een zaak behandelen:* To handle a **case/matter**.
*   *Dat is een andere zaak:* That is a different **thing/matter**.
*   *Een zaak kopen:* To buy a **shop/business**.

### 4. Derivational Morphology
While `zaak` itself is a single morpheme, it frequently acts as the **base** for other words. In these instances, `zaak` remains the root morpheme, and new morphemes are added to it.

*   **`zaak` + `-je` (Diminutive Suffix):**
    *   *Word:* `zaakje`
    *   *Meaning:* A small matter / a small shop.
    *   *Morphemes:* 2 (`zaak` + `-je`)
*   **`zaak` + `-man` (Noun Compound):**
    *   *Word:* `zaakman`
    *   *Meaning:* Businessman.
    *   *Morphemes:* 2 (`zaak` + `man`)
*   **`ver-` (Prefix) + `zaak` + `-en` (Verb Suffix):**
    *   *Word:* `verzaak` (rare/archaic) or more commonly `verwaarlozen` (different root).
    *   *Note:* There is no common verb *verzaaken* in modern Dutch, but `in-` + `zaak` exists in legal contexts (*in de zaak*).

### Summary
From a morphological standpoint, **`zaak`** is an **indivisible lexical root**. It is a **free morpheme** that functions as a noun. Its linguistic significance lies in its etymological journey from a specific legal term ("accusation") to a general term for "thing" or "business," a shift preserved in its cognates across the Germanic language family.
