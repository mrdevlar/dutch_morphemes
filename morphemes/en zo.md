From a strict linguistic perspective, there is an important distinction to make before analyzing the morphemes: **"`en zo`" is not a single word.** It is a **phrase** (specifically a syntactic combination) consisting of two separate words.

In morphology (the study of word structure), we analyze the internal parts of *words*. Since "`en`" and "`zo`" are two distinct lexical items, we must analyze them individually as **monomorphemic words** (words consisting of a single morpheme).

Here is the breakdown from a linguistic perspective:

### 1. The Word: `en`
*   **Morphological Status:** Monomorphemic (1 morpheme).
*   **Morpheme:** `/en/`
*   **Type:** **Free Morpheme** (it can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** Coordinating Conjunction (*voegwoord*).
*   **Function:** It signals addition or coordination between two elements (e.g., *brood **en** kaas* / bread **and** cheese).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *en*, from Proto-Germanic *\*und*.
*   **Inflection:** It is invariant (it does not change form based on tense, number, or gender).

### 2. The Word: `zo`
*   **Morphological Status:** Monomorphemic (1 morpheme).
*   **Morpheme:** `/zo/`
*   **Type:** **Free Morpheme**.
*   **Part of Speech:** Adverb (*bijwoord*) or Demonstrative (*aanwijzend voornaamwoord*).
*   **Function:** It indicates manner, degree, or points to a specific state ("so," "thus," "like this").
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *sō*, from Proto-Germanic *\*swē*.
*   **Inflection:** It is invariant.

### 3. The Combination: `en zo`
Since this is a phrase, we look at it through **Syntax** and **Semantics** rather than internal morphology.

*   **Syntactic Structure:** Conjunction (`en`) + Adverb (`zo`).
*   **Semantic Meaning:** In isolation, it literally means "and so." However, in common usage, it functions as an **idiomatic phrase** meaning "and so on," "etc.," or "and things like that."
    *   *Example:* "Ik heb appels, peren, **en zo**." (I have apples, pears, **and so on**.)
*   **Morphological Note:** Because they are written as two separate words, they do not form a compound word. They remain two distinct morphological units joined syntactically.

### 4. Comparison with the Compound Word: `enzovoorts`
It is common for learners to confuse "`en zo`" with the actual compound word **`enzovoorts`** (or `enzovoort`). If you are interested in the morphology of the *concept* of "etc." in Dutch, `enzovoorts` is the morphologically complex version:

*   **Word:** `enzovoorts`
*   **Breakdown:** `en` + `zo` + `voorts`
*   **Morphemes:**
    1.  `en` (and)
    2.  `zo` (so)
    3.  `voorts` (further/forward)
*   **Status:** This is a **compound word** (samenstelling). In this specific case, the three morphemes have fused into a single lexical unit.

**Conclusion:**
In the phrase "`en zo`", there are **two morphemes total**, but they belong to **two separate words**. There is no internal morphological structure to break down within either word itself, as both are indivisible free morphemes.
