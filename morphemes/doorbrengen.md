From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`doorbrengen`** is a compound verb consisting of three distinct morphemes. It is a classic example of a **separable verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*) in Dutch morphology.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:
> **door** + **breng** + **-en**

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. `door` (Prefix / Particle)
*   **Morpheme Type:** Separable Prefix (or Prepositional Particle).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *dur*, cognate with the German *durch* and the English **through**.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Through," "across," or "during."
*   **Function:** In this compound, *door* modifies the action of the verb. It adds a sense of duration or completion through a period of time.
*   **Stress:** In the infinitive form *doorbrengen*, the primary stress typically falls on the root verb (*door-BREN-gen*), which signals that it is a separable verb.

#### B. `breng` (Verb Root / Stem)
*   **Morpheme Type:** Lexical Root (Stem).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *brengan*, cognate with the German *bringen* and the English **bring**.
*   **Meaning:** "To bring," "to carry," or "to convey."
*   **Conjugation Class:** It is a **strong verb** (Class 3).
    *   Infinitive: *brengen*
    *   Past Tense: *bracht*
    *   Past Participle: *gebracht*
*   **Function:** This is the core semantic unit that carries the primary action of the word.

#### C. `-en` (Infinitive Suffix)
*   **Morpheme Type:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** This suffix marks the verb as an **infinitive** (the dictionary form). It indicates that the verb is not conjugated for person or number in this specific form.
*   **Phonology:** In rapid speech, this final *-en* is often reduced to a schwa sound /ə/ or dropped entirely in colloquial Dutch.

---

### 3. Syntactic and Semantic Behavior

#### The "Separable" Nature
From a syntactic perspective, *doorbrengen* is a **separable verb**. This means that in a main clause, the prefix *door* detaches from the root *brengen* and moves to the end of the sentence.

*   **Infinitive:** *Ik wil de tijd **doorbrengen**.* (I want to spend the time.)
*   **Main Clause:** *Ik **breng** de tijd **door**.* (I spend the time.)
*   **Past Participle:** *Ik heb de tijd **doorgebracht**.* (Note: The *ge-* is inserted between the prefix and the stem, which is the standard rule for separable verbs.)

#### Semantic Composition (Literal vs. Idiomatic)
Linguistically, this is a case of **semantic bleaching** and **idiomatization**.

1.  **Literal Meaning:** "To bring through."
    *   *Example:* *De brandweer bracht de brand door de muur.* (The fire brigade brought the fire through the wall.)
2.  **Idiomatic Meaning:** "To spend (time)."
    *   *Example:* *We brachten de avond door.* (We spent the evening.)
    *   **Analysis:** Here, the physical action of "bringing" is abstracted. You are not physically moving time; rather, you are "conveying" yourself through a duration. This is similar to the English idiom "to **pass** time," where "pass" implies movement through a period.

### Summary
*   **`door`**: A separable prefix indicating "through" or "during."
*   **`breng`**: The root verb meaning "to bring."
*   **`-en`**: The infinitive marker.
*   **Structure**: Compound Verb (Preposition + Verb).
*   **Grammar**: Separable Verb (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).

The word is a perfect example of how Dutch morphology combines simple Germanic roots to create complex, idiomatic meanings through compounding.
