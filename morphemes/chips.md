From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`chips`** is a fascinating example of a **loanword** (leenwoord) that has undergone partial assimilation into Dutch morphology.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of its morphemes.

### 1. Visual Morphemic Breakdown
While the word appears to be a single unit in modern usage, etymologically and structurally, it can be divided into two morphemes:

> **`chip`** + **`-s`**

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Root: `chip`
*   **Type:** Lexical Morpheme (Root).
*   **Origin:** English.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the English word *chip*, which comes from the Old English *cipp* (meaning a small piece or fragment).
*   **Meaning in Dutch:**
    *   **Primary Meaning (Food):** A thin slice of fried potato (what Americans call "chips" and British call "crisps").
    *   **Secondary Meaning (Tech/Gaming):** In Dutch, the singular form *een chip* is also used, but it refers to a computer microchip or a poker chip, not the food.
*   **Morphological Status:** In the context of the food, the root `chip` is **not** productive on its own in Dutch. You cannot say *"een chip"* to mean one potato crisp. Therefore, for this specific meaning, the root is bound to the suffix.

#### B. The Suffix: `-s`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Morpheme (Plural Marker).
*   **Origin:** English (borrowed along with the root).
*   **Function:** In English, `-s` marks the plural of regular nouns (e.g., *cat* → *cats*).
*   **Function in Dutch:**
    *   Dutch also uses `-s` as a plural marker (e.g., *auto* → *auto's*).
    *   However, in the word *chips*, the `-s` is **lexicalized**. This means it has become part of the word's identity rather than a grammatical marker that can be removed.
    *   Unlike native Dutch words where you can often find a singular form (e.g., *auto* vs. *auto's*), for the food item, the form *chips* is treated as a **pluralia tantum** (a noun that only exists in the plural form) or a collective noun.

### 3. Linguistic Context & Assimilation

#### Loanword Status (Leenwoord)
`Chips` is a **direct loanword**. It was borrowed from English (likely in the mid-20th century) without translation.
*   **Phonology:** The pronunciation is adapted to Dutch phonology (often pronounced with a Dutch 'ch' sound /x/ or /k/, depending on the region, rather than the English /tʃ/).
*   **Orthography:** It retains the English spelling.

#### Grammatical Gender
*   **Article:** **`de`** (Common gender).
*   **Example:** *De chips zijn op.* (The chips are gone.)
*   **Note:** Even though it ends in `-s` (which often indicates plural in Dutch), it takes the singular definite article *de* when referring to the concept of the food generally, but it often governs a **plural verb** (*zijn* instead of *is*), reflecting its origin as a plural noun.

#### Semantic Shift
There is a distinct semantic difference between the English and Dutch usage regarding the `-s`:
*   **English:** *Chips* (plural) vs. *Chip* (singular).
*   **Dutch:** *Chips* (food) vs. *Chip* (computer/poker).
    *   If a Dutch speaker wants to refer to a single potato crisp, they usually say *een stukje chips* (a piece of chips) rather than *een chip*.

### 4. Summary Table

| Morpheme | Type | Origin | Function in Dutch |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`chip`** | Root | English | Denotes the object (potato slice). Bound morpheme for this meaning. |
| **`-s`** | Suffix | English | Plural marker. Lexicalized (part of the singular concept in Dutch). |
| **`chips`** | **Word** | **Loanword** | **De-noun. Often functions as a pluralia tantum (plural only).** |

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`chips`** is a **borrowed plural noun** that has been reanalyzed in Dutch. While it consists of the root `chip` and the plural suffix `-s`, Dutch speakers often treat the entire string `chips` as a single, unanalyzable lexical unit for the food item. It is a classic example of how loanwords can retain their foreign morphological structure while adapting to the grammatical rules (gender, verb agreement) of the borrowing language.
