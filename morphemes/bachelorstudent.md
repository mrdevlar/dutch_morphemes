From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`bachelorstudent`** is a **compound noun** (*samengesteld zelfstandig naamwoord*). It is formed through the process of **compounding**, where two independent lexical items are joined to create a new word with a specific meaning.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two **free morphemes** (morphemes that can stand alone as words) joined together without a linking element.

> **`bachelor`** + **`student`**

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. `bachelor`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (Noun).
*   **Origin:** Loanword (from English/French, ultimately from Latin *baccalaureus*).
*   **Function:** **Modifier (Attribute).** In this compound, the first element modifies the second. It specifies the *type* or *level* of the student.
*   **Semantic Role:** It denotes the specific academic degree level (undergraduate) the student is pursuing.
*   **Note:** In Dutch, unlike English, "bachelor" does not carry the meaning of "unmarried man" in this context; it is strictly an academic term.

#### B. `student`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (Noun).
*   **Origin:** Loanword (from Latin *studens*, "one who studies").
*   **Function:** **Head (Huid).** In Dutch compounds, the head is almost always the **rightmost** element. The head determines the grammatical category (noun) and the core meaning of the whole word.
*   **Semantic Role:** It denotes the person (an individual engaged in study).
*   **Inflection:** If this word were pluralized, the plural suffix would attach to this morpheme (e.g., *bachelorstudent**en***), confirming it is the head.

### 3. Structural Analysis

*   **Compound Type:** **Endocentric Compound.**
    *   The meaning of the whole word is a hyponym (a specific type) of the head. A *bachelorstudent* is a specific type of *student*.
*   **Linking Element:** **Zero Morpheme.**
    *   Dutch compounds often use a linking vowel (like *-s-*, *-e-*, or *-en-*) between the two parts (e.g., *liefdesbrief* or *kinderwagen*). However, in this specific case, there is no linking element inserted between `bachelor` and `student`. They are juxtaposed directly.
*   **Word Order:** **Modifier-Head.**
    *   Dutch follows a Germanic structure where the modifier precedes the head (Left-to-Right: Attribute $\rightarrow$ Head).

### 5. Linguistic Context
While both parts are loanwords, they are fully **integrated** into Dutch morphology.
*   **Orthography:** They are written as one closed word (though hyphenated forms like *bachelor-student* are sometimes seen for clarity).
*   **Pluralization:** As mentioned, the plural is formed on the head: *de bachelorstudent* $\rightarrow$ *de bachelorstudent**en***.
*   **Possession:** If possessive, the suffix attaches to the head: *de bachelorstudent**s** auto* (colloquially) or more formally *de auto van de bachelorstudent*.

In summary, **`bachelorstudent`** is a **Noun-Noun compound** where the international loanword `bachelor` acts as a classifier for the head noun `student`.
