From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`truffelmayonaise`** is a **compound noun** (*samengesteld zelfstandig naamwoord*).

In Dutch morphology, compound nouns are typically formed by joining two or more free morphemes (words that can stand alone) without a linking element.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Segmentation
The word consists of two distinct morphemes:
> **`truffel`** + **`mayonaise`**

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. `truffel`
*   **Morpheme Type:** Free morpheme / Root.
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Truffle" (the edible fungus).
*   **Etymology:** Borrowed from French *truffe*, which derives from Italian *tartufo*, ultimately from Latin *tuber* (swelling/lump).
*   **Function in Compound:** **Modifier.** It specifies the type or flavor of the head noun. It narrows the semantic category of the sauce.

#### B. `mayonaise`
*   **Morpheme Type:** Free morpheme / Root.
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Mayonnaise" (the emulsion sauce).
*   **Etymology:** Borrowed from French *mayonnaise* (possibly derived from *Mahón*, a city in Menorca, or the verb *manier* meaning to handle/mix).
*   **Function in Compound:** **Head.** In Dutch (and Germanic languages generally), the right-hand element of a compound is the "head." This means the compound belongs to the category of the head. Therefore, a *truffelmayonaise* is a type of *mayonaise*, not a type of *truffel*.

### 3. Syntactic and Semantic Structure
*   **Compound Type:** **Endocentric.** The meaning of the whole word is a hyponym (a specific subtype) of the head word.
*   **Head Direction:** **Right-headed.** The semantic center is on the right (`mayonaise`).
*   **Linking Element:** **None.** Unlike German (which often uses an *-s-* or *-e-* linking morpheme) or English (which sometimes uses an *-s-*), Dutch compounds usually concatenate nouns directly.
    *   *Incorrect:* `truffelsmayonaise`
    *   *Correct:* `truffelmayonaise`

### 4. Prosody (Stress Pattern)
In Dutch compounds, the primary stress typically falls on the **first element** of the compound. This distinguishes the compound from a noun phrase where stress might fall differently.

### 5. Orthography and Pluralization
*   **Spelling:** In Dutch, compound nouns are written as a single word (closed compound).
*   **Plural:** When pluralized, the suffix is added to the **head** (the last element).
