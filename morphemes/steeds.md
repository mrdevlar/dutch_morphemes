From a linguistic perspective, the analysis of the Dutch word **`steeds`** requires distinguishing between **synchronic** analysis (how the word functions in modern Dutch) and **diachronic** analysis (its historical development and etymology).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes and the linguistic explanation.

### 1. Overview
*   **Word:** `steeds`
*   **Part of Speech:** Adverb
*   **Meaning:** "Always," "continually," or "increasingly" (often used in the phrase *steeds meer* = "more and more").
*   **Pronunciation:** /steːts/

---

### 2. Synchronic Analysis (Modern Dutch)
In the context of modern Dutch grammar and how native speakers process the word today, **`steeds` is considered a monomorphemic word.**

*   **Morpheme:** `steeds` (1)
*   **Type:** Bound Root / Lexicalized Adverb
*   **Explanation:** Modern speakers do not consciously decompose this word into parts. There is no productive noun *`steed`* in contemporary Dutch that means "place" or "standing" which they can combine with a suffix. Therefore, linguistically, it is treated as a single, unanalyzable unit (a fossilized form).

---

### 3. Diachronic Analysis (Historical/Etymological)
When looking at the history of the language (diachrony), **`steeds` is polymorphemic.** It consists of a stem and a suffix that have fused over time.

#### A. The Stem: `steed-`
*   **Origin:** Middle Dutch *steede* / Old Dutch *stēd*.
*   **Root Meaning:** "Place," "standing," or "location."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Proto-Germanic **\*stēdaz**.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   **English:** *Stead* (as in "in my stead" or "homestead").
    *   **German:** *Stätte* (place/site).
    *   **Dutch:** *Stad* (city, originally a "fortified place").
*   **Connection to Verb:** This root is also related to the verb **`staan`** (to stand). Both share the Proto-Indo-European root **\*steh₂-** (to stand). The semantic link is "that which stands" $\rightarrow$ "a place."

#### B. The Suffix: `-s`
*   **Origin:** The adverbial genitive suffix.
*   **Function:** In older Germanic languages, adding *-s* to a noun often created an adverb indicating "at this [noun]" or "by this [noun]."
*   **Comparison:**
    *   *Thans* (now) $\leftarrow$ *thans* (time/place) + *-s*.
    *   *Soms* (sometimes) $\leftarrow$ *som* (sum/part) + *-s*.
    *   *Nergens* (nowhere) $\leftarrow$ *ne* + *ergens* + *-s*.
    *   *Altijd* (always) $\leftarrow$ *al* + *tijd* (time) (Note: *altijd* does not use the *-s* suffix, showing variation in adverb formation).
*   **Meaning in this context:** "At that place" or "In that standing."

---

### 4. Semantic Shift (Grammaticalization)
The most interesting linguistic aspect of `steeds` is how its meaning changed from spatial to temporal.

1.  **Spatial (Original):** "At the same place" (*op dezelfde stede*).
2.  **Temporal (Intermediate):** "Continuously" (staying in the same place implies not moving/leaving).
3.  **Temporal (Modern):** "Always" or "Continually."

This is a classic example of **grammaticalization**, where a concrete spatial concept evolves into an abstract temporal concept.
*   *English Parallel:* The word "always" comes from "all" + "ways" (in all directions/paths), which also implies continuity.
*   *Dutch Parallel:* `Steeds` implies "in the same standing."

### 5. Usage Nuance: "Steeds meer"
In modern usage, `steeds` often appears in the construction **`steeds meer`** (increasingly).
*   **Literal:** "Always more."
*   **Idiomatic:** "More and more."
*   **Linguistic Note:** Here, `steeds` functions as an intensifier of frequency, modifying the comparative adjective `meer`.

### Conclusion
While a modern Dutch speaker treats **`steeds`** as a single, indivisible morpheme (a lexicalized adverb), a historical linguistic analysis reveals it is composed of the noun stem **`steed-`** (place/standing) and the adverbial suffix **`-s`**. The word represents a semantic shift from a spatial concept ("at the same place") to a temporal one ("always").
