From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`levensreddend`** is a **compound adjective** formed through derivation and compounding. It translates to English as "life-saving."

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of the word.

### 1. Visual Segmentation
The word can be segmented into four distinct morphemes:

> **`leven`** + **`-s-`** + **`redd`** + **`-end`**

---

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### **Morpheme 1: `leven`**
*   **Category:** Noun (Substantive).
*   **Meaning:** "Life."
*   **Function:** This is the head of the first constituent of the compound. It specifies *what* is being saved.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Old Dutch *leban*, from Proto-Germanic *\*libaną* (to live). It is cognate with the English verb *to live* and the noun *life* (though English *life* comes from *\*libam*).
*   **Note:** In this compound, it appears in its singular form, not the plural (*levens* as in "lives").

#### **Morpheme 2: `-s-`**
*   **Category:** Linking Morpheme (Interfix).
*   **Function:** This is a **tussenklank** (linking sound) used in Dutch compounding. Historically, this represents the genitive case marker (equivalent to English *'s* or *of*), indicating possession or relation ("life's saving"). In modern Dutch morphology, it is analyzed as a linking element that connects the noun to the following participle.
*   **Phonology:** It prevents a vowel clash and marks the boundary between the noun and the verbal element.

#### **Morpheme 3: `redd`**
*   **Category:** Verb Stem.
*   **Source:** Derived from the verb **`redden`** (to save, to rescue).
*   **Function:** This provides the semantic action of the word.
*   **Morphophonology:** The infinitive is *redden*. The present tense stem is *red-* (e.g., *ik red* - I save). However, when forming the present participle, Dutch orthography requires the doubling of the consonant to maintain the short vowel sound of the stem.
    *   Stem: *red-* (short 'e')
    *   Suffix: *-end*
    *   Result: *reddend* (The `d` is doubled).
*   **Etymology:** From Proto-Germanic *\*redjaną*. It is not cognate with English *save* (Latin origin), but is related to the archaic English *rid* or *rede* (counsel).

#### **Morpheme 4: `-end`**
*   **Category:** Derivational Suffix.
*   **Function:** This forms the **present participle** (*tegenwoordig deelwoord*) of the verb.
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, present participles ending in *-end* function as adjectives.
*   **Comparison:** This is directly equivalent to the English suffix **-ing** (e.g., *saving*).

---

### 3. Grammatical Structure

The word is an **endocentric compound adjective**.

1.  **Base Structure:** Noun (`leven`) + Participle (`reddend`).
2.  **Modification:** The noun modifies the participle. It answers the question: "Saving *what*?" -> "Saving *life*."
3.  **Syntactic Role:** It functions as an attributive adjective (placed before a noun, e.g., *een levensreddende daad* - a life-saving deed) or a predicative adjective (e.g., *De actie was levensreddend* - The action was life-saving).

### 5. Linguistic Context
This word follows a very common pattern in Dutch (and Germanic languages) for creating adjectives from noun-verb combinations. Similar examples include:
*   **`hulpverlenend`** (help-providing / aid-giving)
*   **`tijdswinnend`** (time-winning / time-saving)
*   **`geldverdienend`** (money-earning)

The structure demonstrates the agglutinative tendencies of Dutch morphology, where distinct grammatical units are strung together to create complex meanings without changing the internal structure of the root words significantly.
