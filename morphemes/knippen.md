From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch verb **`knippen`** (to cut, to clip, to snip) can be analyzed as a combination of two distinct morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown:

### 1. The Breakdown
**`knip`** + **`-en`**

| Morpheme | Type | Function | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`knip-`** | **Root / Stem** | Lexical | The core semantic concept: "to cut/snip." |
| **`-en`** | **Suffix** | Inflectional | Marks the verb as the **infinitive** (dictionary form). |

---

### 2. Detailed Explanation

#### A. The Root: `knip-`
*   **Classification:** This is a **lexical morpheme** (specifically, a verbal stem). It carries the primary meaning of the word.
*   **Bound vs. Free:** In modern Dutch, `knip-` is generally a **bound root** when functioning as a verb. You cannot use it in isolation (e.g., you cannot say *"Ik knip"* to mean "I cut" in the present tense without conjugation; you must say *"Ik knip"* [present] or *"Ik knippen"* [incorrect]). However, it appears as a free element in compounds (e.g., *knipbeurt* - a haircut session).
*   **Etymology:** It is of Germanic origin, related to the English word *nip* and the German *kneifen* (to pinch/nip). It is likely onomatopoeic, imitating the sound of scissors closing.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Classification:** This is an **inflectional morpheme**.
*   **Function:** In Dutch grammar, the suffix `-en` is the standard marker for the **infinitive** form of a verb. It indicates the action in its abstract, un-conjugated state (e.g., "to cut").
*   **Allomorphy:** While `-en` is the standard infinitive ending, it can sometimes reduce to just `-n` after a vowel (e.g., *gaan* vs. *gaan* - though *gaan* keeps the *n*, *lopen* keeps the *n*). In the case of `knippen`, the full `-en` is retained.

---

### 3. Orthographic and Phonological Note (The Double 'P')
A strictly morphological breakdown yields `knip` + `en`. However, the written form is **`knippen`** (with a double *p*). This is not a third morpheme, but rather a result of **Dutch orthographic rules** interacting with phonology:

1.  **Vowel Length:** The vowel `i` in `knip` is a **short vowel** (/ɪ/).
2.  **The Rule:** In Dutch spelling, a short stressed vowel in a closed syllable must be followed by a double consonant to prevent the vowel from being read as long.
3.  **Application:** When the suffix `-en` is added, the syllable structure changes. To maintain the short sound of the `i`, the consonant `p` is doubled.
    *   *Phonological:* /knɪp/ + /ən/
    *   *Orthographic:* `knip` + `en` $\rightarrow$ `knippen`

If the root had a long vowel (like *lezen* - to read), the consonant would not double (*lezen*, not *lezzzen*).

### 4. Morphological Class
`Knippen` is classified as a **weak verb** (regular verb). This means it forms its past tense and past participle using dental suffixes (`-te-` / `-d-`) rather than changing its internal vowel (ablaut).

*   **Infinitive:** knippen (`knip` + `-en`)
*   **Past Tense:** knipte (`knip` + `-te`)
*   **Past Participle:** geknipt (`ge-` + `knip` + `-t`)

### Summary
Linguistically, **`knippen`** is a **monomorphemic stem** (`knip-`) combined with an **infinitive suffix** (`-en`). The doubling of the consonant is an orthographic necessity to preserve the short vowel quality of the root, not an additional morphological unit.
