From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`hoest`** is primarily analyzed as a **monomorphemic** word (consisting of a single morpheme), though its analysis becomes slightly more complex depending on whether it is functioning as a noun or a verb.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes.

### 1. The Core Morpheme (The Root)
Regardless of its grammatical function, the core unit of meaning is the root.

*   **Morpheme:** `hoest`
*   **Type:** **Free Morpheme** / **Root**
*   **Meaning:** "Cough" (the act or the sound).
*   **Status:** This is a lexical morpheme. It carries the primary semantic content and cannot be broken down further into smaller meaningful units in Modern Dutch.

---

### 2. Contextual Analysis: Noun vs. Verb
In Dutch, `hoest` exhibits **conversion** (also known as zero-derivation), meaning the same form functions as two different parts of speech without changing its shape.

#### A. As a Noun (*de hoest*)
*   **Structure:** `{hoest}`
*   **Analysis:** This is strictly **monomorphemic**.
*   **Explanation:** Here, `hoest` is the stem of the noun. There are no inflectional suffixes attached in the singular form.
    *   *Example:* "Hij heeft een **hoest**." (He has a cough.)

#### B. As a Verb (*hij hoest*)
*   **Structure:** `{hoest}` + `{t}` (Underlyingly)
*   **Analysis:** This is potentially **bimorphemic** (Root + Inflection), though the suffix is phonologically merged.
*   **Explanation:**
    *   **Root:** `hoest-` (The verb stem).
    *   **Inflection:** `-t` (The 3rd person singular present tense marker).
    *   **Phonological Rule:** In Dutch orthography and phonology, when a verb stem already ends in a `-t` (like *hoest*), the 3rd person singular suffix `-t` is not doubled in writing or pronounced distinctly.
    *   **Underlying Form:** /hoest/ + /t/
    *   **Surface Form:** [hoest]
    *   *Example:* "Hij **hoest**." (He coughs.)

*Note: In the infinitive form (*hoesten*), the morpheme breakdown is clearly `{hoest}` (root) + `{en}` (infinitive marker).*

---

### 3. Etymological Morphology (Historical Perspective)
Looking at the history of the word adds depth to the morphological analysis.

*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*hōstijaną*
*   **Old Dutch:** *hōsten*
*   **Middle Dutch:** *hoesten*
*   **Modern Dutch:** *hoesten* (infinitive) / *hoest* (stem)

Historically, the `-t` sound was part of the root itself (from the Proto-Germanic *\*hōst-*). Therefore, modern linguists often treat the entire string `hoest` as the **root**, rather than analyzing the final `t` as a suffix in the noun form.

### 4. Derivational Potential
While `hoest` itself is a root, it serves as the base for many **derived words** (complex morphemes). This confirms its status as a root morpheme.

*   **`hoest` + `drank`** = *hoestdrank* (Cough syrup)
    *   *Morphemes:* `{hoest}` (Root) + `{drank}` (Root) = Compound Noun.
*   **`hoest` + `bui`** = *hoestbui* (Coughing fit)
    *   *Morphemes:* `{hoest}` (Root) + `{bui}` (Root) = Compound Noun.
*   **`hoest` + `er`** = *hoester* (Cougher - rare, usually *hoestbui*)
    *   *Morphemes:* `{hoest}` (Root) + `{er}` (Agentive Suffix).
