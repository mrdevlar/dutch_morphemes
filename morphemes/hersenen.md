From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`hersenen`** (meaning "brains" or "mind") is a noun that presents a fascinating case of morphological structure, specifically regarding grammatical number and historical derivation.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of its morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation

| Morpheme | Form | Type | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`hersen-`** | Root / Stem | Lexical Morpheme | Carries the core semantic meaning ("brain"). |
| **`-en`** | Suffix | Inflectional Morpheme | Marks the noun as **plural**. |

**Total Morphemes:** 2 (1 Root + 1 Suffix)

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### A. The Stem: `hersen-`
*   **Category:** Noun Root.
*   **Semantics:** Refers to the physical organ (the brain) or, by metonymy, the faculty of intellect/mind.
*   **Usage Note:** In modern standard Dutch, the singular form *`hersen`* is rarely used in isolation. It is primarily found in **compound words** where the plural marker is dropped.
    *   *Examples:* `hersenschudding` (concussion), `hersenvlies` (meninges), `hersencellen` (brain cells).
    *   *Linguistic Implication:* This confirms that `hersen-` is the underlying lexical stem, and the `-en` is an inflectional addition rather than part of the root itself.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Category:** Inflectional Suffix (Plural Marker).
*   **Function:** In Dutch, `-en` is one of the two most common plural markers (the other being `-s`). It attaches to the stem to indicate plurality.
*   **Phonology:** In this word, the `-en` is pronounced as a schwa sound /ə/ (often written as a silent 'e' in casual speech, e.g., *'t hersen*), though in careful speech it is /ən/.

---

### 3. Linguistic Nuance: *Plurale Tantum*

The most significant linguistic feature of `hersenen` is that it functions as a **_plurale tantum_** (Latin for "plural only").

*   **Definition:** A noun that has a plural form but no singular form, or a noun that is grammatically plural even when referring to a single entity.
*   **Application:** While English speakers think of "the brain" as a singular organ, Dutch speakers grammatically treat it as a pair or a collection (`de hersenen`).
    *   *Correct:* `De hersenen werken goed.` (The brains work well.)
    *   *Incorrect/Archaic:* `De hersen werkt goed.`
*   **Historical Context:** This likely stems from the Proto-Germanic root **\*hursan-**, which may have originally been a collective noun or inherently plural in form, referring to the two hemispheres or the convoluted texture of the organ.

### 4. Etymological Context

*   **Origin:** Middle Dutch *hersene*, Old Dutch *hersana*.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   **German:** *Gehirn* (Not a cognate; different root).
    *   **English:** *Brain* (Not a cognate; English "brain" comes from PIE *\*bhrēg-* "to break").
    *   **Frisian:** *hirnen* (Close cognate).
    *   **Old High German:** *hirsana*.
*   **Root Meaning:** The etymological root is debated. Some linguists suggest a connection to a Proto-Germanic root meaning "to turn" or "to churn" (perhaps referring to the texture of the brain), while others suggest a connection to the concept of the "head" or "skull." However, there is no direct link to the English word "head" or "brain."

### 5. Summary

From a morphological standpoint, **`hersenen`** is a **pluralized noun stem**.

1.  **`hersen-`**: The lexical base meaning "brain."
2.  **`-en`**: The inflectional suffix marking plural number.

The word is a classic example of a **_plurale tantum_** in Dutch, where the plural morphology is obligatory for the concept of the brain, distinguishing it from many other body parts in the language (like *hand* / *handen* or *oog* / *ogen*) which have distinct, commonly used singular forms.
