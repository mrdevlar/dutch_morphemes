From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`motivatie`** (meaning "motivation") can be analyzed through **morphology** (the study of word structure) and **etymology** (word origins).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two distinct morphemes:

> **`motiv`** + **`-atie`**

*   **`motiv`**: The lexical root (bound morpheme).
*   **`-atie`**: The derivational suffix (bound morpheme).

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `motiv-`
*   **Type:** Bound Root (Lexical Morpheme).
*   **Meaning:** "Move" or "Motive."
*   **Status in Dutch:** This is a **bound root**, meaning it cannot stand alone as an independent word in modern Dutch. You cannot say *"Ik heb een motiv"* (I have a motive). Instead, Dutch uses the word *motief* for "motive" (which shares the same etymological root but took a different phonological path).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Latin verb **`movere`** (to move). Specifically, it comes from the Latin past participle stem **`mot-`** (as in *motus*, meaning "moved") combined with the adjectival suffix *-ivus* (as in *motivus*, meaning "serving to move").
*   **Function:** It provides the core semantic content of the word. It appears in related Dutch words like *motiveren* (to motivate) and *demotivatie* (demotivation).

#### B. The Suffix: `-atie`
*   **Type:** Derivational Suffix.
*   **Meaning:** Indicates an action, process, or result of an action.
*   **Etymology:** Borrowed from the Latin suffix **`-atio`** (genitive *-ationis*), which was transmitted into Dutch via French (*-ation*).
*   **Function:** It performs **nominalization**. It takes a verbal concept (the act of motivating) and turns it into an abstract noun.
*   **Productivity:** This is a highly productive suffix in Dutch for words of international/Latin origin.
    *   *Examples:* *educatie* (education), *creatie* (creation), *operatie* (operation), *situatie* (situation).

---

### 3. Derivational History & Grammar

To understand the full linguistic picture, it helps to see where *motivatie* sits in the word family:

1.  **Latin:** *movere* (verb: to move) $\rightarrow$ *motivus* (adj: motive).
2.  **Dutch Verb:** *motiveren* (to motivate).
    *   *Stem:* *motiv-*
    *   *Infinitive Suffix:* *-eren* (a common Dutch suffix for verbs of Latin/French origin).
3.  **Dutch Noun:** *motivatie* (motivation).
    *   *Stem:* *motiv-*
    *   *Noun Suffix:* *-atie*

**Grammatical Properties:**
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun (*Zelfstandig naamwoord*).
*   **Gender:** Common (*de-woord*). You say *de motivatie*.
*   **Plural:** *motivaties*.
*   **Stress Pattern:** The stress falls on the penultimate syllable (the 'a'): *moti-**va**-tie*. This is standard for Dutch words ending in *-atie*.

### 4. Comparison with English
Because both Dutch and English are Germanic languages that borrowed heavily from Latin and French, the morphological structure is nearly identical:

*   **Dutch:** `motiv` + `atie`
*   **English:** `motiv` + `ation`

The only difference is the phonological realization of the suffix (*-atie* vs. *-ation*), which reflects the different sound shifts that occurred in the two languages.
