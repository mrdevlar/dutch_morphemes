From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`dwingen`** (meaning "to force," "to compel," or "to coerce") can be analyzed through **morphology**, the study of the internal structure of words.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of its morphemes.

### 1. The Morphemic Breakdown

In Modern Standard Dutch, `dwingen` is analyzed as consisting of **two morphemes**:

| Morpheme | Segment | Type | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | `dwing-` | **Root / Stem** | Lexical meaning (the core concept of "forcing"). |
| **2** | `-en` | **Inflectional Suffix** | Grammatical marker indicating the **infinitive** form. |

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### A. The Root: `dwing-`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Lexical Root).
*   **Function:** This carries the primary semantic content of the word. It defines the action itself.
*   **Phonology:** In this specific verb, the root ends in a velar nasal /ŋ/ (represented by *ng*).
*   **Stem Variation:** In Dutch conjugation, this stem undergoes **ablaut** (vowel gradation) because `dwingen` is a **strong verb** (Class 3).
    *   Present tense stem: *dwing-* (e.g., *ik dwing* - I force)
    *   Past tense stem: *dwong-* (e.g., *ik dwong* - I forced)
    *   Past participle stem: *gedwong-* (e.g., *gedwongen* - forced)
    *   *Note:* While the vowel changes, the consonantal skeleton (`d-w-ng`) remains the identifier of the root.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** This is the standard marker for the **infinitive** in Dutch verbs.
*   **Grammar:** It distinguishes the base form of the verb from conjugated forms.
    *   *Ik dwing* (I force) — No suffix (1st person singular present).
    *   *Om te dwingen* (To force) — Suffix present (Infinitive).
*   **Phonology:** In rapid speech or certain dialects, this final *-n* may be elided (dropped), sounding like *dwinge*, but in standard orthography and morphological analysis, it is treated as a distinct morpheme.

### 3. Derivational Relationships (Word Family)

While `dwingen` itself is not a compound word, it is morphologically related to other words through **derivation**. This helps contextualize the root `dwing-`.

**1. The Noun: `dwang`**
*   **Structure:** `dwing` + `-` (zero derivation) + `a` (vowel change).
*   **Relationship:** There is a historical and morphological relationship between the verb *dwingen* and the noun *dwang* (force/coercion).
*   **Analysis:** In many Germanic languages, there is a pattern where a verb root creates a noun via vowel change (ablaut) or suffixation. Here, the verb *dwingen* and noun *dwang* share the same etymological root.

**2. The Adjective/Participle: `gedwongen`**
*   **Structure:** `ge-` (prefix) + `dwing` (root) + `-en` (suffix).
*   **Analysis:** This is the past participle form used as an adjective (e.g., *een gedwongen glimlach* - a forced smile).
    *   `ge-`: Past participle marker.
    *   `-en`: Adjectival/Participial ending.

**3. The Separable Verb: `aandwingen`**
*   **Structure:** `aan-` (prefix) + `dwingen` (verb).
*   **Analysis:** This is a compound verb where the prefix `aan-` adds a directional or completive nuance (to enforce/compel specifically).
    *   *Ik dwing het af* (I enforce it).

### 4. Etymological Context

To fully understand the morphemes, it helps to look at the **Proto-Germanic** origin:
*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*dwenganą*
*   **Old Dutch:** *dwangian*
*   **Middle Dutch:** *dwengen*
*   **Modern Dutch:** *dwingen*

The word is cognate with the German verb **`zwingen`**. In German, the initial *d-* shifted to *z-* (High German consonant shift), but the morphological structure (Root + Infinitive ending) remains identical.

### Summary
Linguistically, **`dwingen`** is a **monolexemic** verb (one dictionary entry) composed of **two morphemes**:
1.  **`dwing-`**: The lexical root carrying the meaning of "force."
2.  **`-en`**: The grammatical suffix marking the infinitive.

It is a **strong verb**, meaning its past tense is formed by changing the internal vowel (*i* to *o*) rather than adding a dental suffix (like *-te* or *-de*), which is a key feature of its morphological paradigm.
