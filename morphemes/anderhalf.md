From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`anderhalf`** (meaning "one and a half" or "1.5") is a **compound word** formed from two distinct free morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Morphemic Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:

> **ander** + **half**

*   **Morpheme 1:** `ander` (Adjective/Numeral)
*   **Morpheme 2:** `half` (Adjective/Noun)
*   **Word Class:** Quantifier / Numeral (functioning as an adjective or adverb).

---

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### Morpheme 1: `ander`
*   **Meaning:** "Other," "second," or "different."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*anþeraz**. It is cognate with the English word **other** and the German word **ander**.
*   **Function in this compound:** In modern isolation, *ander* usually means "different" (e.g., *een ander boek* = a different book). However, in this specific numerical context, it functions as an **ordinal** concept meaning "the next" or "the second."
*   **Semantic Logic:** It implies the existence of a "first" unit (which is the number 1, though the word *een* is elided/omitted). Therefore, *ander* refers to the *second* unit in the sequence.

#### Morpheme 2: `half`
*   **Meaning:** "Half" (0.5).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*halbaz**. It is cognate with the English word **half** and the German word **halb**.
*   **Function in this compound:** It denotes the fractional part of the quantity.
*   **Grammatical Category:** Historically a noun, but in this compound, it functions adjectivally to modify the implied unit.

---

### 3. Semantic Composition (The Logic of the Compound)
The combination of these two morphemes creates a specific semantic meaning that differs slightly from a literal translation.

*   **Literal Construction:** "Other-half."
*   **Actual Meaning:** "One and a half."
*   **The Logic:** The construction relies on an implied "one."
    1.  You have **one** whole unit (implied).
    2.  You have half of the **other** (second) unit.
    3.  Therefore: *Ander* (the second/other unit) + *half* (of it) = 1.5.

This is similar to how English uses "one and a half," but Dutch condenses the "one and" into the prefix *ander-*.

---

### 4. Inflectional Morphology (The *-e* ending)
While the base form is **`anderhalf`**, Dutch morphology requires inflection based on the gender and number of the noun it modifies. This introduces a third, **inflectional morpheme**: the suffix **`-e`**.

*   **Base Form (Neuter/Indefinite):** `anderhalf`
    *   *Example:* *Een anderhalf uur.* (An hour and a half).
    *   *Context:* Used with neuter nouns (*het*-words) or when the noun is omitted.
*   **Inflected Form (Common Gender/Attributive):** `anderhalve`
    *   *Example:* *De anderhalve meter.* (The one-and-a-half meters).
    *   *Morphology:* `ander` + `half` + **`-e`** (inflectional suffix).
    *   *Rule:* In Dutch, adjectives preceding a common gender noun (*de*-words) in the singular usually take an *-e* ending.

### 5. Comparison with English
From a typological perspective, this highlights a difference in how the two languages handle fractional numbers:

*   **English:** Analytic/Periphrastic. It uses a phrase: *one* + *and* + *a* + *half*.
*   **Dutch:** Synthetic/Compounding. It fuses the concept into a single lexical item: *anderhalf*.
