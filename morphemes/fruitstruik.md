From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`fruitstruik`** is a **compound noun** (samenstelling). It consists of two distinct free morphemes joined together to create a new semantic unit.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:
> **`fruit`** + **`struik`**

There is **no linking morpheme** (such as *-s-* or *-en-*) between the two elements, which is common in Dutch compounding, though not universal.

---

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### Morpheme A: `fruit`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Lexical Noun).
*   **Status:** Loanword (Exogenous).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *fruit*, borrowed from Old French *fruit*, which traces back to Latin *fructus* ("enjoyment," "produce," or "fruit").
*   **Semantics:** Refers to the edible produce of plants. In modern Dutch, *fruit* is often used for culinary or garden contexts, whereas the native Dutch word *vrucht* is often used for biological contexts or abstract meanings (e.g., *de vruchten van zijn werk* — the fruits of his labor).
*   **Function in Compound:** **Modifier.** It specifies the *type* of the head noun.

#### Morpheme B: `struik`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Lexical Noun).
*   **Status:** Native Germanic (Endogenous).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *struyc*, related to Old High German *struoh*. It is cognate with the English word *shrub* (via Low German influence) and related to the verb *strew* (in the sense of spreading branches).
*   **Semantics:** Refers to a woody plant smaller than a tree, typically with multiple stems.
*   **Function in Compound:** **Head.** In Dutch compounds, the rightmost element is the head. Therefore, a *fruitstruik* is a type of *struik* (bush), not a type of *fruit*.

---

### 3. Linguistic Features of the Compound

#### A. Morphological Process: Endocentric Compounding
This is an **endocentric compound**. The meaning of the whole word is a hyponym (a specific subtype) of the head morpheme.
*   *Logic:* A `fruitstruik` is a `struik`.
*   *Contrast:* If it were exocentric (like *spilbaard* or *blauwbaard*), the meaning would not be a type of the head.

#### B. Syntactic Structure: Noun + Noun (N+N)
Both constituents are nouns. In Dutch, when two nouns are compounded, the first noun acts adjectivally to modify the second noun.
*   *Structure:* [Noun (Attribute)] + [Noun (Head)]

#### C. Phonology: Stress Pattern
In Dutch compounds, the primary lexical stress typically falls on the **first element**.
*   **Pronunciation:** **FRUIT**-struik (not fruit-STRUIK).
*   This distinguishes it from a phrase. If you said "fruit struik" as two separate words, the stress would likely shift or be equal, but as a compound, the stress anchors on *fruit*.

#### D. Orthography
*   **Closed Compound:** The two morphemes are written as a single word without a space or hyphen.
*   **No Linking Element:** Unlike many Dutch compounds that require an interfix (e.g., *liefde**s**verklaring* or *bloemen**en**tuin*), *fruitstruik* does not take an *-s-* or *-en-*. This is partly because *fruit* ends in a vowel and *struik* begins with a consonant, making the transition phonetically smooth without a buffer.

### Comparison with English
The structure is identical to the English compound **"fruit bush"**.
*   **English:** *fruit* (Modifier) + *bush* (Head).
*   **Dutch:** *fruit* (Modifier) + *struik* (Head).

Both languages utilize the same morphological strategy (Noun-Noun compounding) to create the same semantic concept, highlighting the shared Germanic syntactic roots, despite the Dutch word *fruit* being a Romance loanword.
