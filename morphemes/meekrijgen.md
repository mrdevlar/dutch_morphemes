From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`meekrijgen`** is a complex verb formed through derivation and compounding. It is classified as a **separable verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of its components.

### 1. Morphemic Breakdown

The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

| Morpheme | Type | Form | Function/Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`mee-`** | Free Morpheme (Particle) | Prefix/Particle | "With," "along," "together." |
| **`krijg-`** | Free Morpheme (Root) | Verb Stem | "Get," "receive," "obtain." |
| **`-en`** | Bound Morpheme | Inflectional Suffix | Marks the infinitive form. |

**Full Segmentation:** `mee` + `krijg` + `-en`

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Particle: `mee-`
*   **Linguistic Category:** Prepositional Particle / Adverbial Particle.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the preposition *mee* (with), cognate with German *mit* and English *with* (via Proto-Germanic *\*miþ*).
*   **Semantic Role:** It adds a **directional** or **associative** meaning to the verb. It implies accompaniment, addition, or inclusion.
*   **Syntactic Behavior:** In Dutch, this is a **separable prefix**. In a main clause, it detaches from the verb and moves to the end of the sentence (e.g., *Ik **krijg** het **mee***). In subordinate clauses or infinitive constructions, it remains attached (e.g., *...om het **meekrijgen***).

#### B. The Root: `krijg-`
*   **Linguistic Category:** Lexical Root (Verb Stem).
*   **Etymology:** From Middle Dutch *krijgen*. Historically related to *krijg* (war/struggle), implying "to gain through effort," though modern usage is broader.
*   **Semantic Role:** The core lexical meaning is acquisition, reception, or obtaining.
*   **Conjugation:** It is an irregular (strong) verb.
    *   Infinitive: *krijgen*
    *   Past Tense: *kreeg*
    *   Past Participle: *gekregen*

#### C. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Linguistic Category:** Inflectional Suffix.
*   **Function:** This is the standard marker for the **infinitive** form of Dutch verbs. It carries no lexical meaning of its own but signals the grammatical mood (non-finite).
*   **Phonology:** In this specific word, the vowel sound of the root (*krijg*) and the suffix blend smoothly, though in some dialects, the *g* might be softened or dropped before the *e*.

### 3. Semantic Composition (How the meaning is built)

Linguistically, the meaning of `meekrijgen` is **compositional but idiomatic**. It is not simply "to get" + "with." The particle modifies the root to create specific nuances:

1.  **Literal (Accompaniment):** To obtain something while moving or to take something along.
    *   *Example:* "Ik heb de sleutel meekregen." (I got the key along with me / I was given the key to take.)
2.  **Perceptual (Cognitive):** To perceive, notice, or understand something (often information).
    *   *Example:* "Heb je dat bericht meekregen?" (Did you catch that message? / Did you get that info?)
3.  **Inclusion:** To include someone in an activity or group.
    *   *Example:* "We moeten de kinderen meekrijgen." (We need to get the kids involved / take the kids along.)
4.  **Coping (Reflexive/Idiomatic):** To manage or keep up with a situation.
    *   *Example:* "Ik krijg het niet meer mee." (I can't keep up anymore / I can't handle it anymore.)

### 4. Syntactic Structure (Morphosyntax)

From a syntactic perspective, `meekrijgen` demonstrates the **V2 (Verb Second)** rule and **Separable Verb** rule of Dutch grammar.

*   **Infinitive:** *Ik wil het **meekrijgen**.* (I want to get it along.)
*   **Main Clause:** *Ik **krijg** het **mee**.* (I get it along.)
    *   Here, the finite verb (*krijg*) moves to the second position, and the particle (*mee*) is stranded at the end of the clause.
*   **Subordinate Clause:** *...dat ik het **mee** **krijg**.* (Note: In subordinate clauses, the finite verb goes to the very end, but the particle usually attaches to it again in spoken language, or stays separated depending on dialect/standard. Standard written Dutch often keeps them separated in the final position: *...dat ik het **mee** **krijg**.*)

### Summary
Linguistically, **`meekrijgen`** is a **separable compound verb**. It consists of a directional particle (`mee-`) modifying a lexical root of acquisition (`krijg-`), inflected for the infinitive (`-en`). The combination creates a polysemous verb where the core meaning of "obtaining" is shifted by the particle to imply "obtaining *in addition*," "obtaining *alongside*," or "obtaining *perceptually*."
