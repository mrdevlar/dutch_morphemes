From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`zoals`** is a **compound conjunction** (or preposition) formed from two distinct morphemes. While it functions as a single lexical unit in modern Dutch, its internal structure reveals its etymological history and semantic composition.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. The Morphemes

The word consists of two free morphemes that have fused over time:

#### **Morpheme 1: `zo`**
*   **Part of Speech:** Adverb.
*   **Meaning:** "So," "thus," "in this manner," or "to this degree."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *so*, Old Dutch *so*, and Proto-Germanic **\*swē*.
*   **Cognates:** English *so*, German *so*, Swedish *så*.
*   **Function in Compound:** It establishes the **manner** or **degree** of the comparison. It points to a specific way of being or acting.

#### **Morpheme 2: `als`**
*   **Part of Speech:** Conjunction / Preposition.
*   **Meaning:** "If," "than," or "as."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Middle Dutch *als*, Old Dutch *als*, and Proto-Germanic **\*alē* (related to "all" or "whole").
*   **Cognates:** English *else* (historically related), German *als*.
*   **Function in Compound:** It introduces the **comparison**, **condition**, or **equivalence**.

---

### 2. Semantic Composition (How they combine)

When combined, the literal semantic composition is roughly **"so + as"** or **"in this manner + as."**

*   **Literal Logic:** "In the way that..."
*   **Modern Meaning:** "Like," "as," or "such as."

The combination creates a **simile** or an **exemplification**. It signals that the subject shares qualities with the object following the word.
*   *Example:* `Hij is slim, zoals zijn vader.` (He is smart, **as** his father.)
*   *Example:* `Dieren, zoals honden.` (Animals, **such as** dogs.)

---

### 3. Linguistic Status: Lexicalization

While `zo` and `als` can exist independently, `zoals` is a **lexicalized compound**.

*   **Synchronic View (Modern Usage):** In contemporary Dutch, `zoals` is treated as a single grammatical unit (a conjunction). You cannot insert other words between them when used in this specific sense.
*   **Diachronic View (Historical Usage):** Historically, these were separate words used in a correlative structure. Over centuries, they fused into a single orthographic and phonological unit.

**Crucial Distinction: Fused vs. Separated**
Linguistically, it is important to distinguish between the fused conjunction `zoals` and the separated comparative structure `zo ... als`.

1.  **Fused (`zoals`):** Used for **exemplification** or **simile**.
    *   *Dutch:* `Zoals ik al zei...` (**As** I already said...)
    *   *Structure:* Single unit.
2.  **Separated (`zo ... als`):** Used for **equality** in comparisons (equative).
    *   *Dutch:* `Hij is **zo** groot **als** jij.` (He is **as** tall **as** you.)
    *   *Structure:* Two separate morphemes framing an adjective.

### 4. Syntactic Function

In the sentence structure, `zoals` functions primarily as a **subordinating conjunction**. It introduces a subordinate clause that provides a comparison or example.

*   **Main Clause:** `Ik eet fruit` (I eat fruit)
*   **Subordinate Clause:** `**zoals** appels` (such as apples)
*   **Combined:** `Ik eet fruit, **zoals** appels.`

### Conclusion
From a morphological standpoint, **`zoals`** is a **compound** formed by the adverb **`zo`** and the conjunction **`als`**. While etymologically transparent (meaning "so-as"), it has undergone **lexicalization**, meaning it is now stored in the mental lexicon of Dutch speakers as a single functional word used to draw comparisons or provide examples.
