From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`vegetariër`** (vegetarian) is a fascinating example of a loanword that has been fully integrated into Dutch morphology. It contains a specific orthographic feature (the trema) that signals a morpheme boundary.

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes, moving from the surface structure to the etymological roots.

### 1. Orthographic Note: The Trema (`ë`)
Before analyzing the morphemes, it is crucial to note the **trema** (diaeresis) on the letter **`ë`**.
*   **Function:** In Dutch orthography, the trema indicates that the vowel it sits on is pronounced in a separate syllable from the preceding vowel.
*   **Pronunciation:** It prevents the sequence `ie` from being read as a long vowel /iː/ (as in *bier*). Instead, it forces a hiatus: /i/ + /ə/.
*   **Phonetic:** /veː.ɣə.taː.**ri**.ˈ**ɛr**/
*   **Morphological Significance:** The trema visually marks the boundary between the stem ending in `-i` and the suffix beginning with `-e`.

### 2. Surface Morphological Segmentation
In modern Dutch morphology, the word is typically segmented based on its current stem and agentive suffix.

**`vegetari-`** + **`-ër`**

*   **`vegetari-` (Stem):** This is the base form of the word used to denote the concept of vegetarianism.
    *   *Evidence:* We see this stem in the related noun **`vegetarisme`** (vegetarianism).
    *   *Note:* There is a slight allomorphic variation here. Before the consonant-initial suffix `-isme`, the stem is `vegetar-`. Before the vowel-initial suffix `-ër`, the stem extends to `vegetari-`.
*   **`-ër` (Agentive Suffix):** This is a productive Dutch suffix used to denote a person who performs an action or adheres to a specific practice (similar to English *-er* as in *baker* or *worker*).
    *   *Variation:* In loanwords of Latin/French origin, this often appears as `-iër` to maintain the stress pattern and vowel quality, but morphologically, the agentive function is carried by the `-ër` part.

### 3. Etymological / Deep Morphological Analysis
To understand *why* the word is structured this way, we must look at its etymological layers. The word is a loan from English (*vegetarian*), which itself comes from Latin.

**`veget-`** + **`-ar-`** + **`-i-`** + **`-ër`**

1.  **`veget-` (Root):**
    *   **Origin:** Latin *vegetus* (meaning "lively," "vigorous," or "animated").
    *   **Semantic Shift:** In the 19th century, this root became associated with *vegetables* (plants) via the word *vegetable*. In this context, it signifies "plant-based."
2.  **`-ar-` (Thematic/Connecting Element):**
    *   **Origin:** Latin *-arius* (suffix indicating "pertaining to").
    *   **Function:** It links the root to the agentive suffix. It creates the concept of "pertaining to plants."
3.  **`-i-` (Connecting Vowel):**
    *   **Origin:** Part of the English suffix *-arian* (from Latin *-arius* + *-an*).
    *   **Function:** In the Dutch adaptation, this vowel is preserved to maintain the syllable structure of the original loanword.
4.  **`-ër` (Agentive Suffix):**
    *   **Origin:** Dutch adaptation of the English *-an* / French *-ien*.
    *   **Function:** Denotes "a person who."

Analyzing the morphemes helps explain related words in the Dutch lexicon:

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`vegetariër`** is a **loanword** that has been morphologically adapted to Dutch rules. The **trema (`ë`)** is the key indicator that the word is not a native Germanic compound but a loanword where the final syllable structure was preserved. The segmentation **`vegetari-` + `-ër`** reflects the Dutch agentive pattern, while the deeper breakdown **`veget-` + `-ar-` + `-i-` + `-ër`** reveals its Latin and English heritage.
