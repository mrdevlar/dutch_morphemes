### 3. Detailed Linguistic Analysis

#### A. Type of Morpheme: Free Root
*   **Free:** The morpheme can stand alone as an independent word. You do not need to add anything to it to make it a valid word in a sentence (e.g., *De beer slaapt* / "The bear sleeps").
*   **Root:** It is the core lexical unit. It carries the primary semantic content ("large omnivorous mammal"). It is not derived from a prefix or a suffix.
*   **Lexical:** It belongs to the open class of words (nouns), meaning new words can be added to this category, and it carries specific dictionary meaning rather than grammatical function.

#### B. Orthography vs. Morphology (The `ee` digraph)
A common point of confusion for learners is the spelling `ee`.
*   **Phonologically:** The `ee` represents a single long vowel sound (/eː/).
*   **Morphologically:** The `ee` is **not** two morphemes. It is a **digraph** (two letters representing one sound). It does not indicate plurality or tense in this specific word.
    *   *Contrast:* In English, "bees" has a root `bee` + plural suffix `s`. In Dutch `beer`, the `e` is part of the root itself.

#### C. Inflectional Context
To prove that `beer` is a single root morpheme, we can look at how it changes when grammatical markers are added. The root remains constant while suffixes are attached:

*   **Singular:** `beer` (Root only)
*   **Plural:** `beer` + `en` = **`beren`** (Root + Plural Suffix)
*   **Diminutive:** `beer` + `tje` = **`beertje`** (Root + Diminutive Suffix)

Because `beer` remains intact in these variations, linguists identify it as the underlying root morpheme.
