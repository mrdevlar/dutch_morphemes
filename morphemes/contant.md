From a linguistic perspective, the analysis of the Dutch word **`contant`** requires distinguishing between its **synchronic** structure (how it functions in modern Dutch) and its **diachronic** structure (its historical etymology).

Here is the breakdown and explanation.

### 1. Synchronic Analysis (Modern Dutch)
In contemporary Dutch morphology, **`contant`** is treated as a **monomorphemic root**.

*   **Word:** `contant`
*   **Morphemes:** 1 (The root itself)
*   **Part of Speech:** Adjective (e.g., *contant geld* = cash money) / Noun (e.g., *de contanten* = the cash).
*   **Analysis:** Modern Dutch speakers do not perceive internal boundaries within this word. You cannot add the prefix *be-* or the suffix *-ig* to it productively (e.g., *becontanten* or *contantig* are not words). Therefore, linguistically, it functions as a single, indivisible lexical unit in the current language system.

### 2. Diachronic Analysis (Etymology & Historical Morphology)
To understand the internal structure, we must look at the word's origin. **`Contant`** is a loanword from French, which in turn derives from Latin.

#### A. The French Source
The word comes from the French **`comptant`**.
*   **French Verb:** *compter* (to count).
*   **French Suffix:** *-ant* (Present participle ending, equivalent to English *-ing*).
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Counting" or "That which is counted."

#### B. The Latin Root
The French *compter* derives from the Latin verb **`computare`**.
*   **Prefix:** `com-` (meaning "together" or "thoroughly").
*   **Root:** `putare` (meaning "to think," "to reckon," "to prune," or "to count").
*   **Latin Suffix:** `-are` (infinitive verb marker).

#### C. The Semantic Shift
How did "to count" become "cash"?
1.  **`Computare`** (Latin): To calculate or count up.
2.  **`Comptant`** (French): In a commercial context, this referred to money that was **counted out immediately** at the time of the transaction (as opposed to credit or a bill of exchange).
3.  **`Contant`** (Dutch): Adopted into Dutch with the specific meaning of "ready money" or "cash."

### 3. Orthographic Adaptation
When the word entered Dutch, it underwent a phonological and orthographic change typical of French loanwords in Dutch:
*   **French:** *comptant* (pronounced with a nasal 'on' sound, spelled with 'mp').
*   **Dutch:** *contant* (The 'mp' cluster was simplified to 'n' before the 't', and the nasal vowel was adapted to standard Dutch pronunciation).

### 4. Derivational Morphology (Noun Formation)
While `contant` is an adjective, it is frequently nominalized (turned into a noun) in Dutch. In this specific usage, a second morpheme appears:

*   **`contant`** (Root: cash/cash-like)
*   **`-en`** (Inflectional Suffix: Plural marker)
*   **Result:** `contanten` (The cash / The bills and coins).
