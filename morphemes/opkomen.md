From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`opkomen`** is a classic example of a **separable compound verb** (*scheidbaar werkwoord*).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of its components.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two distinct morphemes:

1.  **`op`** (Prefix / Particle)
2.  **`komen`** (Root Verb)

**Structure:** `[Prefix] + [Verb Stem]`

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Prefix: `op`
*   **Category:** Separable verbal particle (derived from a preposition/adverb).
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Up," "on," or "upon."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*upp*, Old Dutch *up*. It is cognate with the English word *up* and the German *auf*.
*   **Function:** In this context, `op` acts as a **directional** or **aspectual** modifier. It changes the direction or the nature of the action performed by the root verb.
    *   *Directional:* Moving upward (e.g., the sun rising).
    *   *Aspectual:* Initiating an action or bringing something into existence (e.g., a problem arising).

#### B. The Root: `komen`
*   **Category:** Verb (Infinitive).
*   **Meaning:** "To come."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*kwemaną*, Old Dutch *kuman*. It is cognate with the English word *come* and the German *kommen*.
*   **Function:** This is the **lexical core** of the word. It carries the primary semantic weight regarding movement or arrival.

---

### 3. Syntactic Behavior: Separability
In Dutch morphology and syntax, `opkomen` is classified as a **separable verb**. This is a crucial linguistic feature.

*   **In the Infinitive:** The morphemes are joined (`opkomen`).
    *   *Example:* "Ik wil **opkomen**." (I want to come up.)
*   **In a Main Clause:** The prefix `op` separates from the verb and moves to the end of the sentence. The conjugated verb (`kom`) stays in the second position.
    *   *Example:* "Ik **kom** morgen **op**." (I am coming up tomorrow.)
*   **In the Past Participle:** The separable prefix is placed *before* the participle marker `ge-`.
    *   *Example:* "Het is **op**ge**komen**." (It has come up / arisen.)

*Note: This differs from inseparable verbs (like `bezoeken` - to visit), where the prefix never moves.*

---

### 4. Semantic Composition (Polysemy)
The combination of these two morphemes creates a polysemous word (a word with multiple related meanings). The specific meaning depends on the context in which the morphemes interact:

1.  **Physical Movement (Literal):**
    *   *Meaning:* To rise or move upward.
    *   *Context:* "De zon komt op." (The sun comes up / rises.)
2.  **Emergence (Abstract):**
    *   *Meaning:* To appear, to arise, or to occur.
    *   *Context:* "Er komt een probleem op." (A problem comes up / arises.)
3.  **Advocacy (Idiomatic):**
    *   *Meaning:* To stand up for someone or something.
    *   *Context:* "Hij komt op voor zijn rechten." (He stands up for his rights.)
    *   *Linguistic Note:* Here, the `op` implies an active, defensive posture rather than physical movement.
4.  **Participation (Political):**
    *   *Meaning:* To vote or show up for an election.
    *   *Context:* "De opkomst was hoog." (The turnout [coming up] was high.)

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`opkomen`** demonstrates the agglutinative tendency of Germanic languages to combine prepositions with verbs to create specific directional meanings. Its status as a **separable verb** dictates its syntax, while the interaction between the directional particle `op` and the motion verb `komen` allows for a wide range of semantic interpretations from physical rising to abstract advocacy.
