From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`drinken`** is a standard example of **inflectional morphology**. It consists of two distinct morphemes.

Here is the breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Segmentation
The word can be segmented as follows:

> **drink** + **en**

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Root (Stem): `drink-`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (Lexical root).
*   **Function:** This is the core semantic unit of the word. It carries the primary meaning ("to consume liquid").
*   **Category:** Verb stem.
*   **Linguistic Note:** In Dutch morphology, the stem is the form to which inflectional endings are attached. While `drink` cannot stand alone as a complete word in modern Dutch (unlike in English, where "drink" is the infinitive), it is the base form used for conjugation (e.g., *ik drink*, *hij dronk*).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*drinkaną**. It is cognate with the English word *drink* and the German word *trinken*.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Bound morpheme (Inflectional suffix).
*   **Function:** This suffix serves two primary grammatical functions in Dutch:
    1.  **Infinitive Marker:** It marks the verb as the citation form (the dictionary form). In Dutch, the standard infinitive ending is *-en*.
    2.  **Present Tense Plural:** It is also the ending used for the first, second, and third person plural in the present tense (e.g., *wij drinken* = we drink).
*   **Category:** Inflectional. It does not change the part of speech (it remains a verb); it only changes the grammatical form.
*   **Phonology:** In this specific word, the suffix is pronounced as a schwa followed by a nasal /ən/ (IPA: /ˈdrɪŋ.kə(n)/). In some dialects or rapid speech, the vowel may be elided, leaving just the nasal /n/, but morphologically it remains the *-en* suffix.

### 3. Summary Table

| Morpheme | Segment | Type | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Root** | `drink-` | Free / Lexical | Carries the core meaning ("to drink"). |
| **Suffix** | `-en` | Bound / Inflectional | Marks the infinitive form (and plural present). |

### 4. Contextual Nuance: Nominalization
It is worth noting that in Dutch, the infinitive form can easily be nominalized (turned into a noun) by adding a definite article, such as in the phrase *het drinken* ("the drinking").
*   In this context, the morphological structure remains **`drink` + `-en`**, but the syntactic category shifts from Verb to Noun. The suffix `-en` is retained from the verbal infinitive form.

### 5. Comparison with English
*   **Dutch:** `drink` + `en` (Two morphemes in the infinitive).
*   **English:** `drink` (One morpheme in the infinitive).
*   **German:** `trink` + `en` (Two morphemes in the infinitive).

This highlights that while English has largely lost the infinitive suffix in the standard form, Dutch and German retain the Proto-Germanic structure where the infinitive is marked by a suffix.
