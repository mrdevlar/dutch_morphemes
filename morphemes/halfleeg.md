From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`halfleeg`** is a classic example of a **compound adjective**. Below is a detailed morphological breakdown, semantic analysis, and grammatical context.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two distinct **free morphemes** joined together to form a single lexical unit.

*   **`half`** + **`leeg`**

There are no bound morphemes (affixes like prefixes or suffixes) attached to the root in this specific base form.

---

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The First Morpheme: `half`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Lexical).
*   **Part of Speech:** In this context, it functions as an **adverbial modifier** within the compound, though historically and etymologically it is an adjective or numeral.
*   **Meaning:** "50%," "not whole," or "partially."
*   **Function:** It acts as the **modifier**. It specifies the degree or extent of the state described by the second morpheme.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *\*halbaz*. Cognates include English *half* and German *halb*.

#### B. The Second Morpheme: `leeg`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (Lexical).
*   **Part of Speech:** Adjective.
*   **Meaning:** "Empty," "void," or "containing nothing."
*   **Function:** It acts as the **head** of the compound. In Dutch compounds, the semantic head usually determines the part of speech of the whole word. Since `leeg` is an adjective, `halfleeg` is an adjective.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *\*laikaz*. Cognates include English *lack* (semantic shift) and German *leer*.

---

### 3. Word Formation Process: Compounding
The process used to create this word is **Endocentric Compounding**.

*   **Closed Compound:** Unlike English, which often writes this concept as two separate words ("half empty"), Dutch orthography requires compound words to be written as one solid unit (`halfleeg`).
*   **Head-Modifier Structure:**
    *   **Head:** `leeg` (The core concept is "emptiness").
    *   **Modifier:** `half` (The qualifier).
    *   **Result:** The word describes a specific type of "emptiness."
*   **Stress Pattern:** In Dutch compounds, the primary stress typically falls on the **first element**.
    *   Pronunciation: **`HÁLF`**leeg (not half`LEEG`). This distinguishes it from a phrase where stress might be distributed differently.

---

### 4. Grammatical Inflection (Morphosyntax)
While the base form is `halfleeg`, Dutch adjectives undergo inflection depending on their syntactic position. This is a crucial part of understanding the morphology of the word in context.

1.  **Predicative Use (No Inflection):**
    *   *Sentence:* "De fles is **halfleeg**." (The bottle is half-empty.)
    *   *Morphology:* The compound remains in its base form.
2.  **Attributive Use (Inflected):**
    *   *Sentence:* "Het is een **halflege** fles." (It is a half-empty bottle.)
    *   *Morphology:* An inflectional suffix **`-e`** is added to the end of the compound.
    *   *Note:* The suffix attaches to the *entire compound*, not just the head (`leeg`). You do not say `half-leeg-e`; the boundary between `half` and `leeg` is invisible to the inflectional rule.

---

### 5. Semantic Compositionality
The meaning of `halfleeg` is **compositional**.
*   **Literal:** 50% empty.
*   **Pragmatic:** In actual usage, it rarely means exactly 50%. It functions as a scalar adjective meaning "partially empty" or "not full."
*   **Antonym:** The opposite compound is `halfvol` (half-full).

### Conclusion
Linguistically, `halfleeg` demonstrates the Dutch preference for **agglutinative compounding** (joining words without changing their internal form) to create precise descriptive terms. It combines a degree modifier (`half`) with a state adjective (`leeg`) into a single orthographic and phonological unit, which then behaves grammatically as a standard Dutch adjective regarding inflection.
