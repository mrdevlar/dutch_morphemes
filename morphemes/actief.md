From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`actief`** (meaning "active") can be analyzed through morphology (structure), etymology (origin), and phonology (sound).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes and their linguistic functions.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word is bimorphemic, consisting of a root and a derivational suffix:

> **`act`** + **`-ief`**

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Root: `act-`
*   **Type:** Bound Root (Lexical Morpheme).
*   **Function:** Carries the core semantic meaning ("doing," "driving," "action").
*   **Status in Dutch:** In modern Dutch, `act-` is not a free morpheme (you cannot use it alone as a word). It is a **bound root** that must attach to a suffix to form a valid word.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Latin verb ***agere*** ("to do, to drive, to act").
    *   The past participle of *agere* is *actus*.
    *   In Dutch, the noun form is **`actie`** (action), which shares the same root.
*   **Phonology:** The `c` is pronounced as a hard /k/ sound (/ɑk/).

#### B. The Suffix: `-ief`
*   **Type:** Derivational Suffix.
*   **Function:** Changes the part of speech from a verbal/nominal concept to an **adjective**. It indicates a quality or tendency related to the root.
*   **Etymology:** Corresponds to the Latin adjectival suffix ***-ivus*** (as in *activus*).
*   **Productivity:** This is a highly productive suffix in Dutch for loanwords of Latin or Greek origin. It creates a pattern recognizable to Dutch speakers, similar to:
    *   *negatief* (negative)
    *   *positief* (positive)
    *   *creatief* (creative)
    *   *passief* (passive)
*   **Phonology:** In Dutch orthography, `ie` represents the long close front unrounded vowel /i/. The `f` is /f/. The stress typically falls on the syllable containing the suffix (ac-**TIEF**).

### 3. Etymological Path
The word traveled through several languages before entering Dutch:
1.  **Latin:** *activus* (from *actus* + *-ivus*).
2.  **French:** *actif* (The French form influenced the spelling and pronunciation significantly).
3.  **Dutch:** *actief* (Adopted during the period of high Latin/French cultural influence, likely 17th–18th century).

### 4. Syntactic and Inflectional Behavior
Although the word is a loan, it behaves like a standard Dutch adjective regarding inflection. This proves that the suffix `-ief` has been fully integrated into Dutch morphology.

*   **Predicative:** *Hij is **actief**.* (He is active.)
*   **Attributive (Indefinite):** *een **actief** kind.* (an active child.)
*   **Attributive (Definite):** *het **actieve** kind.* (the active child.)
    *   *Note:* The suffix `-e` is added here, which is a separate inflectional morpheme for adjectives preceding a noun.
*   **Comparative:** *actief**er**.* (more active)
*   **Superlative:** *actief**st**.* (most active)

### 5. Related Morphological Family
The root `act-` generates a family of words in Dutch, demonstrating the productivity of this specific morpheme:

*   **`actie`** (Noun): Action.
*   **`activeren`** (Verb): To activate (Root `act` + Suffix `-er` + Suffix `-en`).
*   **`activiteit`** (Noun): Activity (Root `act` + Suffix `-iv` + Suffix `-iteit`).
*   **`actief`** (Adjective): Active.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`actief`** is a **loanword** that has been **morphologically integrated** into Dutch. While its components (`act-` and `-ief`) are not native Germanic morphemes, Dutch speakers analyze them as a root and a suffix because they follow the productive pattern of other Latinate adjectives in the language (like *negatief*).
