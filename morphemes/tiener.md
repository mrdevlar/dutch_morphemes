From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`tiener`** (meaning "teenager") is a fascinating example of a loanword that has been nativized and re-analyzed within Dutch morphology.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into two distinct morphemes:

> **`tien`** + **`-er`**

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `tien`
*   **Form:** `tien`
*   **Part of Speech:** Numeral (Cardinal Number)
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Ten"
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*tehuną*, cognate with the English word "ten" and the German "zehn".
*   **Semantic Shift:** In this specific context, the meaning of "ten" undergoes a **metonymic shift**. It does not refer to the number 10, but rather to the decade of life associated with numbers ending in the Dutch suffix *-tien* (thirteen to nineteen: *dertien* to *negentien*).
    *   *Linguistic Note:* In English, the source word "teen" comes from the Old English suffix *-tīne* (found in *thirteen*, *fourteen*). In Dutch, however, the root is the free morpheme *tien* (the number 10 itself), which is then associated with the "teen" years.

#### B. The Suffix: `-er`
*   **Form:** `-er`
*   **Type:** Derivational Suffix
*   **Function:** Agentive / Nominalizer
*   **Meaning:** "One who [does/is associated with]..."
*   **Usage in Dutch:** The suffix *-er* is highly productive in Dutch. It typically attaches to verbs to create nouns denoting the doer of the action (e.g., *bakken* "to bake" → *bakker* "baker"; *werken* "to work" → *werker* "worker").
*   **Usage in `tiener`:** Here, it attaches to a numeral rather than a verb. It nominalizes the concept, turning the age group ("ten-ness") into a person ("one of the ten-ers"). This parallels English usage (e.g., *New Yorker*, *Londoner*), where *-er* denotes a person associated with a place or concept.

### 3. Etymological Context & Loanword Status

While `tiener` looks like a native Dutch construction, it is historically a **loanword** (specifically a loan translation or calque) from English.

*   **Source:** English *teenager* (coined in the 1940s).
*   **Adaptation:**
    1.  **Phonological:** English *teen* /tiːn/ was adapted to Dutch orthography and phonology as *tien* /tin/.
    2.  **Morphological:** The English word consists of *teen* + *age* + *er*. The Dutch word dropped the morpheme for "age" (*-age* / *-leeftijd*) and kept only the root and the agentive suffix.
    3.  **Re-analysis:** Dutch speakers parsed the word not as a foreign chunk, but as a native structure: *tien* (ten) + *-er* (person).

### 4. Summary Table

| Morpheme | Type | Origin | Function | Meaning in Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`tien`** | Root | Germanic (Numeral) | Semantic Base | Refers to the "teen" years (13–19) |
| **`-er`** | Suffix | Germanic (Agentive) | Derivational | Denotes a person belonging to that group |

### 5. Linguistic Conclusion
The word **`tiener`** demonstrates **morphological nativization**. Although borrowed from English, it was stripped of the English suffix *-age* and fitted into the Dutch derivational pattern of **Numeral + *-er***. This makes it a transparent compound in modern Dutch, even though its semantic origin relies on the English concept of the "teen" suffix.
