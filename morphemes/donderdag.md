From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`donderdag`** (Thursday) is a classic example of a **Germanic compound noun**. It is formed through the combination of two free morphemes.

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into two distinct morphemes:

> **donder** + **dag**

*   **Morpheme 1:** `donder` (Noun)
*   **Morpheme 2:** `dag` (Noun)

There is **no linking morpheme** (such as an *-s-* or *-e-*) between them in Modern Dutch, though historically, a genitive marker was implied.

---

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. `donder`
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Thunder."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Proto-Germanic **\*þunraz** (thunder).
    *   Cognates include Old English *þunor*, Old High German *donar*, and Gothic *þunrus*.
*   **Semantic Shift (Metonymy):** While the word literally means the physical phenomenon of thunder, in the context of the days of the week, it functions as a **metonym** for the deity associated with thunder. In Continental Germanic mythology, this god was **Donar** (the equivalent of the Norse god Thor).
*   **Phonology:** /ˈdɔn.dər/

#### B. `dag`
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Literal Meaning:** "Day."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from the Proto-Germanic **\*dagaz** (day).
    *   Cognates include Old English *dæg*, German *Tag*, and Swedish *dag*.
*   **Phonology:** /dɑx/ (in isolation), though in compounds, the final consonant often undergoes devoicing or assimilation depending on the dialect.

---

### 3. Compound Structure
*   **Type:** Endocentric Compound.
    *   The head of the compound is the second element (`dag`). The word refers to a specific type of "day."
    *   The first element (`donder`) acts as a modifier, specifying *which* day it is.
*   **Stress Pattern:** In Dutch compounds, primary stress typically falls on the first element.
    *   Pronunciation: **DÓN**-der-dag.

---

### 4. Historical & Cultural Context (Interpretatio Germanica)
To fully understand the morphology, one must look at the history of the calendar.

*   **Latin Origin:** The Romans named the day *dies Jovis* ("Day of Jupiter"). Jupiter was the king of gods and associated with thunder and lightning.
*   **Germanic Translation:** When Germanic peoples adopted the Roman seven-day week, they replaced the Roman gods with their own equivalents. This process is known as **Interpretatio Germanica**.
    *   They substituted Jupiter with their own thunder god, **Donar** (or Thor).
    *   Therefore, *dies Jovis* became *Donarestag* (Old High German) or *Þunresdæg* (Old English).
*   **Modern Dutch Form:** Over time, the genitive marker (the *-s-* or *-es-* indicating possession, as seen in Old English *Þunresdæg*) was dropped in the modern Dutch form.
    *   Old High German: *Donarestag* (Donar + **es** + tag)
    *   Modern Dutch: *donderdag* (Donder + tag)

This morphological structure is shared across the West Germanic languages, highlighting their common ancestry:

### Summary
Linguistically, **`donderdag`** is a **noun-noun compound** where the modifier `donder` (thunder/Donar) specifies the head noun `dag` (day). It represents a historical calque (loan translation) of the Latin *dies Jovis*, preserving the Proto-Germanic vocabulary for "thunder" and "day" while reflecting the cultural substitution of the Roman god Jupiter with the Germanic god Donar.
