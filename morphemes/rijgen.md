From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch verb **`rijgen`** can be analyzed through its morphological structure, etymology, and grammatical function.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two morphemes:

> **`rijg`** + **`-en`**

*   **`rijg-`**: The verbal stem (root).
*   **`-en`**: The infinitive suffix.

---

### 2. Analysis of the Stem: `rijg-`

*   **Type:** Free lexical morpheme (verbal root).
*   **Meaning:** The core semantic content refers to the action of threading, lacing, or stringing objects together in a line.
*   **Phonology:**
    *   **`r`**: Alveolar trill or tap [r].
    *   **`ij`**: Diphthong [ɛi] (similar to the 'ay' in English "day" but starting with a more open vowel).
    *   **`g`**: In standard Dutch, this is a voiced velar fricative [ɣ] (soft 'g') in the stem, though it may devoice to [x] in certain positions or dialects.
*   **Productivity:** This stem is productive in Dutch. It can form compounds and derivatives, such as:
    *   *doorrijgen* (to thread through)
    *   *oprijgen* (to string up / to lace up)
    *   *rijgdraad* (threading thread)

#### Etymology and Cognates
The stem `rijg-` is historically significant and connects to the concept of a "row" or "line."

*   **Old Dutch:** *rigian* / *reigian*.
*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*reigōną* (to string, to draw, to row).
*   **Cognates:**
    *   **Dutch:** *rij* (row/line). The semantic link is strong: *rijgen* is the action of creating a *rij* (row) of items on a string.
    *   **German:** *reihen* (to arrange in a row), *Reihe* (row/series).
    *   **English:** *rig* (in the sense of rigging a ship, which involves stringing ropes, though the etymology is slightly divergent via Old Norse *rygja*).

---

### 3. Analysis of the Suffix: `-en`

*   **Type:** Inflectional morpheme (bound).
*   **Function:** Infinitive marker.
*   **Grammatical Category:** Verbal.
*   **Explanation:** In Dutch, the suffix `-en` is the standard marker for the **infinitive** form of a verb (equivalent to "to" + verb in English, e.g., "to thread").
    *   *Example:* *rijgen* (to thread), *lopen* (to walk), *zien* (to see).
*   **Variation:** In some dialects or colloquial speech, this may be reduced to `-n` (e.g., *rijgn*), but in standard morphology, it is analyzed as `-en`.

---

### 4. Grammatical Behavior (Conjugation)
While the infinitive is `rijgen`, the morphological structure changes slightly when conjugated, revealing the verb class.

*   **Verb Class:** Weak verb (regular conjugation), but with a vowel alternation in the past tense.
*   **Present:** *ik rijg* (I thread)
*   **Past:** *ik riegde* (I threaded) — *Note the vowel change from `ij` [ɛi] to `ie` [i].*
*   **Past Participle:** *ge-rijg-d* (threaded) — *Note the prefix `ge-` and the suffix `-d`.*

This vowel alternation (*rijg* vs. *rieg*) is a remnant of historical sound changes (specifically related to the interaction of the root vowel with the following consonant in the past tense suffix *-d-*), distinguishing it from completely invariant verbs.

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`rijgen`** is a **monomorphemic stem** (`rijg-`) combined with a **verbal infinitive suffix** (`-en`). It is a weak verb derived from a Proto-Germanic root meaning "to draw" or "to row," semantically linked to the Dutch noun *rij* (row), reflecting the action of arranging items in a line on a string or thread.
