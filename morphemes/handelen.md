From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`handelen`** (meaning "to act," "to deal," or "to trade") can be analyzed through **morphology** (the study of word structure).

Here is the breakdown of the morphemes, followed by an explanation of their function and etymology.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation

The word can be segmented into three distinct morphemes:

> **`hand`** + **`-el-`** + **`-en`**

*   **`hand`**: Root (Free Morpheme)
*   **`-el-`**: Derivational Suffix (Bound Morpheme)
*   **`-en`**: Inflectional Suffix (Bound Morpheme)

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `hand`
*   **Type:** Free Morpheme (can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** Noun.
*   **Meaning:** "Hand" (the body part).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*handuz**. It is cognate with the English word *hand*, the German *Hand*, and the Old Norse *hönd*.
*   **Function:** This provides the core semantic meaning. The action described by the verb is conceptually linked to the use of the hand.

#### B. The Derivational Suffix: `-el-`
*   **Type:** Bound Morpheme (cannot stand alone).
*   **Function:** **Verbalizer / Nominalizer**.
*   **Analysis:** This is the most complex part of the word.
    *   Historically, the suffix `-el-` (often appearing as `-el` or `-el-`) serves to transform a concrete noun into an abstract noun or a verb indicating an activity associated with that noun.
    *   In this specific case, it creates the stem **`handel-`**.
    *   **Semantic Shift:** It shifts the meaning from the physical object ("hand") to the **activity** performed by the object ("handling," "dealing," "conduct").
    *   *Note:* This same suffix appears in the noun **`de handel`** (the trade/commerce) and **`de handeling`** (the action/ceremony).
*   **Linguistic Category:** This is a **derivational** suffix because it changes the lexical category (or meaning) of the root, creating a new word stem.

#### C. The Inflectional Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Bound Morpheme.
*   **Function:** **Infinitive Marker**.
*   **Analysis:** In Dutch grammar, the standard ending for the infinitive form of a verb is `-en`.
*   **Linguistic Category:** This is an **inflectional** suffix. It does not change the core meaning of the word but marks its grammatical form (non-finite verb).
*   **Conjugation:** When conjugated, this `-en` is often dropped or modified (e.g., *ik handel*, *hij handelt*, *wij handelden*).

---

### 3. Semantic Development (Meaning Evolution)

The transition from "hand" to "handelen" illustrates a classic process of **semantic broadening** and **metonymy**:

1.  **Literal:** Originally, the word implied the physical act of **handling** objects with one's hands.
2.  **Abstract:** It broadened to mean **acting** or **behaving** generally (since hands are the primary tools of human agency).
    *   *Example:* "Hij handelt verstandig" (He acts wisely).
3.  **Commercial:** It specialized further to mean **trading** or **dealing** in goods (historically, trade involved the physical handling of goods and handshakes).
    *   *Example:* "Handelen in goederen" (Trading in goods).

### 4. Cross-Linguistic Comparison (English)

It is helpful to compare this with English to understand the morphological relationship:

*   **Dutch:** `hand` + `el` + `en` = **`handelen`** (to act/trade)
*   **English:** `hand` + `le` = **`handle`** (to touch/manage)

Both languages use the Germanic root `hand`. However, English uses the suffix `-le` (a frequentative or diminutive suffix) to create the verb *handle*. Dutch uses `-el-` + `-en`.
*   Interestingly, the Dutch noun **`handel`** (trade) is cognate with the English noun **`handel`** (archaic/dialect for trade) or the verb **`handle`** in the sense of "to manage."
