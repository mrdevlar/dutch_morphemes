From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`liggen`** is an infinitive verb meaning "to lie" (in the sense of reclining or being located).

Here is the morphological breakdown and explanation of its components.

### 1. Morphemic Segmentation
At the synchronic level (how the word is structured in modern Dutch), the word consists of two morphemes:

> **lig** + **-en**

*   **lig**: The lexical root (stem).
*   **-en**: The infinitive suffix.

*(Note: The double 'g' in the spelling is an orthographic feature, not a separate morpheme. See section 3 below.)*

---

### 2. Detailed Explanation of Morphemes

#### A. The Root: `lig-`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme / Lexeme root.
*   **Meaning:** This carries the core semantic content of the word: "to be in a horizontal position," "to recline," or "to be situated."
*   **Phonology:** In the underlying form, this morpheme ends in a voiced velar fricative (spelled *g*, pronounced roughly like the *ch* in Scottish *loch* or a soft *g* depending on dialect).
*   **Grammatical Class:** It is the stem of a **strong verb** (Class 3). This means it forms its past tense through vowel change (ablaut) rather than adding a *-d* or *-t* suffix.
    *   *Present:* ik **lig** (I lie)
    *   *Past:* ik **lag** (I lay)
    *   *Past Participle:* **gelegen** (lain)

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional suffix.
*   **Function:** It marks the **infinitive** form of the verb. In Dutch, almost all verbs take *-en* (or *-n* after a vowel) to form the dictionary form.
*   **Etymology:** This suffix derives from the Proto-Germanic infinitive ending *\*-an*, which evolved into Old Dutch *-an* and eventually Modern Dutch *-en*.
*   **Pronunciation:** In many dialects, this final *-n* is often dropped or nasalized in casual speech (e.g., *ligge*), but it remains morphologically present.

---

### 3. The Orthographic Issue: Why "gg"?
A common point of confusion is the double consonant. Why is it spelled *liggen* instead of *ligen*?

*   **Morphological Reality:** The stem is **`lig`**.
*   **Orthographic Rule:** Dutch spelling follows the "short vowel rule." If a syllable contains a short vowel (like the /ɪ/ in *lig*), and that syllable is closed by a consonant, the consonant must be doubled in writing to prevent the vowel from being read as long.
*   **The Process:**
    1.  Stem: *lig* (short vowel /ɪ/)
    2.  Add Suffix: *-en*
    3.  Result: *lig-en*
    4.  Spelling Adjustment: To keep the /ɪ/ sound short in the first syllable, the *g* is doubled.
    5.  Final Spelling: **liggen**

Therefore, the second *g* is not a morpheme; it is an **orthographic gemination** used to preserve the phonological quality of the root morpheme.

---

### 4. Diachronic Perspective (Etymology)
Looking at the history of the word provides further linguistic context:

*   **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** *\*legh-* (to lie down).
*   **Proto-Germanic:** *\*ligjaną*.
    *   The *\*j* in the Germanic form caused a palatalization effect in some languages, but in Dutch, it solidified into the *g* sound.
*   **Old Dutch:** *liggen*.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   English: *lie* (Note: English lost the *g* sound but kept the meaning).
    *   German: *liegen*.
    *   Swedish: *ligga*.
