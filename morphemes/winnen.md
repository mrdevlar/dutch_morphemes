From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`winnen`** (meaning "to win") can be analyzed as a verb in its infinitive form. It consists of two primary morphological components: a lexical stem and an inflectional suffix.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of the morphemes:

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
Visually and structurally, the word is segmented as follows:

> **`winn`** + **`en`**

### 2. Detailed Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Stem: `winn-`
*   **Type:** Lexical Morpheme (Root/Stem)
*   **Function:** Carries the core semantic meaning of the word ("to win" / "victory").
*   **Linguistic Nuance (Allomorphy):**
    *   Strictly speaking, the underlying root of this verb is **`win-`**. You can see this in the first-person singular present tense: *ik **win*** (I win).
    *   However, in the infinitive form (`winnen`), the stem appears as **`winn-`**.
    *   **Why the double 'n'?** This is due to a **morphophonemic rule** in Dutch orthography. In Dutch, a short, stressed vowel (like the *i* in *win*) must be followed by a double consonant if the syllable is closed.
    *   In the infinitive *winn-en*, the word is divided into two syllables (*win-nen*). The first syllable is closed by the *n*, so the consonant is doubled to indicate that the vowel remains short. If it were spelled *`winen`*, a Dutch speaker might pronounce the *i* as a long vowel (like in *wijn*).
    *   Therefore, `winn-` is an **allomorph** (a variant form) of the root `win-`, conditioned by the following suffix.

#### B. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Morpheme
*   **Function:** Marks the **infinitive** form of the verb.
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, the suffix `-en` (or sometimes just `-n` after a vowel) is the standard marker for the dictionary form of a verb. It indicates that the verb is not conjugated for a specific person or tense.
    *   *Example:* *lopen* (to walk), *eten* (to eat), *winnen* (to win).

### 3. Morphological Classification
*   **Word Class:** Verb (Werkwoord)
*   **Verb Type:** **Strong Verb** (Sterk werkwoord).
    *   While the infinitive ending `-en` is regular, the verb changes its vowel in the past tense, which classifies it as "strong" in Germanic linguistics.
    *   *Present:* ik **win**
    *   *Past:* ik **won**
    *   *Past Participle:* ge**wonnen**
*   **Derivational Potential:** The stem `winn-` is also used to create derived words, such as:
    *   **`winnaar`** (winner): `winn-` + `-aar` (agentive suffix).
    *   **`winnend`** (winning): `winn-` + `-d` (present participle suffix).

### Conclusion
In the word **`winnen`**, the morphological structure is **Stem + Suffix**. The stem **`winn-`** provides the meaning, while the suffix **`-en`** provides the grammatical category (infinitive). The double *n* is not a separate morpheme but a phonological adjustment to the root `win-` to ensure correct pronunciation of the vowel.
