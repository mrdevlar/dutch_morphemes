However, to provide a complete linguistic explanation, we must look at both its **synchronic morphology** (current structure) and its **diachronic morphology** (historical development).

Here is the breakdown:

### 1. Synchronic Analysis (Current Structure)

In the modern Dutch language system, `schaars` cannot be broken down into smaller meaningful parts (morphemes) that function independently.

*   **Morpheme:** `schaars`
*   **Type:** **Free Root Morpheme**
*   **Part of Speech:** Adjective
*   **Meaning:** Scarce, rare, in short supply.

**Why is it not broken down further?**
Unlike words such as *on-mogelijk* (un-possible) or *leer-ling* (learn-er), there is no productive prefix or suffix in modern Dutch that can be stripped away from `schaars` to reveal a different core meaning.
*   You cannot remove the *-s* to get a word *`schaar`* that means "scarce" (the word *`schaar`* exists, but it means "scissors" or "shears," which is a different lexical item).
*   You cannot remove *`sch-`* or *`-aars`* to find a meaningful root.

Therefore, for the purpose of modern morphological analysis, the entire word is treated as **one morpheme**.

### 2. Diachronic Analysis (Historical Etymology)

While `schaars` is one morpheme today, historically it evolved from a Proto-Germanic root. Understanding this helps explain the internal structure of the sound and meaning.

*   **Proto-Germanic:** **\*skarsaz**
*   **Old Dutch:** *skars*
*   **Middle Dutch:** *scars*
*   **Cognates:** English *scarce*, German *schier* (archaic/dialectal for "barely/scarce").

**The Semantic Shift:**
The root is related to the verb **`scheren`** (to shear, to cut, to shave).
1.  **Original Concept:** "Cut" or "Cut off."
2.  **Evolution:** If something is "cut off" or "cut short," it becomes limited in quantity.
3.  **Modern Meaning:** "Limited in quantity" $\rightarrow$ **Scarce**.

**The *-s* ending:**
In Proto-Germanic, the *-s* was likely part of the adjectival suffix (similar to the *-s* in English *scarce* or German *-s* in adjectives). Over time, this suffix fused completely with the root. In modern Dutch, this *-s* is no longer a separate suffix; it is an integral part of the root morpheme.
