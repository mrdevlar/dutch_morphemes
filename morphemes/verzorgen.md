From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`verzorgen`** is a complex verb formed through derivational and inflectional morphology. It can be segmented into three distinct morphemes.

Here is the breakdown: **`ver-`** + **`zorg`** + **`-en`**

---

### 1. The Prefix: `ver-`
*   **Type:** Derivational Prefix (Bound Morpheme).
*   **Function:** This is one of the most common prefixes in Dutch (and Germanic languages). It is highly polysemous (has multiple meanings), but in this specific context, it functions as a **causative** or **completive** marker.
*   **Semantic Nuance:**
    *   It often indicates a change of state or a thorough application of the action.
    *   When attached to a noun or root like *zorg*, it transforms the concept of "care" into the active process of "applying care" or "providing for."
    *   It distinguishes the verb from the simpler root verb *zorgen*. While *zorgen* can mean "to worry" or "to provide" (often requiring the preposition *voor*), *verzorgen* implies a more direct, hands-on, or comprehensive act of maintenance or nursing.
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic *\*far-*, cognate with the English prefix *for-* (as in *forbid* or *forgive*, though the meaning has shifted significantly in English).

### 2. The Root: `zorg`
*   **Type:** Lexical Root (Free Morpheme).
*   **Part of Speech:** Originally a Noun (*de zorg* = the care/worry).
*   **Meaning:** Care, concern, worry, or provision.
*   **Semantic Nuance:** This is the core semantic unit. It carries the weight of the definition.
*   **Etymology:** From Middle Dutch *sorghe*, from Old Dutch *sorga*, from Proto-Germanic *\*surgō*.
*   **Cognates:**
    *   **German:** *Sorge* (care/worry).
    *   **English:** *Sorrow* (historically linked to the feeling of care/worry), though the direct cognate *sorg* is archaic in English.
    *   **English:** *Surgeon* (via Latin *chirurgus*, but the Germanic root shares the concept of "working on" or "curing").

### 3. The Suffix: `-en`
*   **Type:** Inflectional Suffix (Bound Morpheme).
*   **Function:** Infinitive Marker.
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, the base form of a verb (the infinitive) almost always ends in *-en* (or *-n* if the stem ends in a vowel).
*   **Note:** While *-en* marks the verb form, the stem *verzorg-* is what carries the lexical meaning. If you conjugate the verb, this suffix changes (e.g., *ik verzorg* [I care for], *hij verzorgt* [he cares for]).

---

### Morphological Composition & Semantics

When combined, the morphemes create a specific semantic shift:

1.  **`zorg`** (Noun): The abstract concept of care.
2.  **`ver-` + `zorg`**: The act of applying that care to an object or person.
3.  **`-en`**: Marks it as a verb in its base form.

**Literal Translation:** "To cause care" or "to apply care."
**Actual Meaning:** To take care of, to look after, to maintain, to provide for, or to groom.

### Related Morphological Forms
To fully understand the morphology, it helps to look at the word family:

*   **`zorg`** (Noun): Care, worry.
*   **`zorgen`** (Verb): To worry OR to provide (usually used with *voor* -> *zorgen voor*).
*   **`verzorgen`** (Verb): To take care of (active maintenance).
*   **`verzorging`** (Noun): Care, maintenance, grooming (formed by adding the nominalizing suffix *-ing* to the verb stem).
*   **`verzorger`** (Noun): Caregiver (formed by adding the agentive suffix *-er*).

> "Zij **verzor**gt de planten."
> *(She **takes care of** the plants.)*

In this sentence, the stem *verzorg-* carries the meaning, while *-t* replaces *-en* to indicate the third-person singular present tense.
