From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`jurist`** is a morphologically complex noun derived from Latin roots, fully integrated into the Dutch lexicon.

Here is the breakdown and explanation of its morphemes.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word can be segmented into two distinct morphemes:

> **`jur-`** + **`-ist`**

### 2. Morpheme Analysis

#### A. The Root: `jur-`
*   **Type:** Bound Lexical Root (Bound Morpheme).
*   **Origin:** Latin *jus* (nominative) / *juris* (genitive), meaning "law," "right," or "oath."
*   **Function:** This morpheme carries the core semantic meaning of the word. It denotes the domain of **law** or **legal rights**.
*   **Status in Dutch:** In modern Dutch, `jur-` is a **bound root**. It cannot stand alone as an independent word (you cannot say *"Ik ben jur"*). It only appears in derived words or compounds.
*   **Phonology:** The *u* is pronounced as a long /y/ (similar to the French *u* or German *ü*), and the *r* is a standard Dutch rhotic.

#### B. The Suffix: `-ist`
*   **Type:** Derivational Suffix.
*   **Origin:** Latin *-ista* (via Greek *-istes*), often entering Dutch through French (*-iste*) or German (*-ist*).
*   **Function:** This is an **agentive suffix**. It converts a noun or root into a noun referring to a person who practices, specializes in, or is concerned with the concept of the root.
*   **Meaning:** "One who does [root]" or "Specialist in [root]."
*   **Productivity:** The suffix *-ist* is highly productive in Dutch (e.g., *pianist*, *artiest*, *socialist*, *journalist*).

### 3. Morphological Process
*   **Process:** **Derivation** (specifically Suffixation).
*   **Base:** The bound root *jur-*.
*   **Output:** A deverbal/de-nominal noun (though the base is a root, not a full word).
*   **Word Class Change:** The suffix turns the abstract concept of "law" into a concrete noun referring to a "person."
    *   *jur-* (Abstract concept: Law) $\rightarrow$ *jurist* (Concrete noun: Legal expert).

### 4. Etymological Context
While the word is analyzed morphologically within Dutch, it is technically a **loanword** (leenwoord).
*   **Latin:** *juris* (law) + *-ista* (practitioner).
*   **French:** *juriste*.
*   **Dutch:** Adopted as *jurist*.

Because it is a loanword, the morphemes are **etymological** rather than purely synchronic. A modern Dutch speaker might intuitively understand the meaning of *-ist* (as in *pianist*), but they may not consciously recognize *jur-* as a separate unit, as it does not exist independently in the language. However, linguists analyze it as two morphemes because the structure is transparent to speakers of related languages (like English *jurist* or German *Jurist*) and related Dutch words.

*   **Word:** `jurist`
*   **Morphemes:** `jur-` (Law) + `-ist` (Specialist/Person)
*   **Literal Meaning:** "One who specializes in law."
*   **Linguistic Classification:** Derived Noun (Agent Noun) containing a Latin bound root.
