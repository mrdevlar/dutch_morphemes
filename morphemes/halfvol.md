From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`halfvol`** is a classic example of a **compound adjective**. Below is a detailed morphological, semantic, and etymological breakdown.

### 1. Morphological Segmentation
The word consists of two distinct free morphemes joined together without a linking element (zero morph).

$$ \text{half} + \text{vol} \rightarrow \text{halfvol} $$

### 2. Analysis of Morphemes

#### Morpheme 1: `half`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** In this context, it functions as an **adverbial modifier** within the compound.
*   **Meaning:** "50%," "not whole," "intermediate."
*   **Function:** It specifies the degree or quantity of the head morpheme. It restricts the meaning of *vol* from "completely full" to "partially full."
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*halbaz**.
    *   *Cognates:* English *half*, German *halb*, Old Norse *helmr*.

#### Morpheme 2: `vol`
*   **Type:** Free morpheme (can stand alone as a word).
*   **Part of Speech:** **Adjective**.
*   **Meaning:** "Full," "containing as much as possible," "filled."
*   **Function:** This is the **head** of the compound. The entire word *halfvol* belongs to the same grammatical category as *vol* (adjective).
*   **Etymology:** Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*fullaz**.
    *   *Cognates:* English *full*, German *voll*, Old English *full*.

### 3. Compound Structure & Syntax
*   **Type of Compound:** **Endocentric**.
    *   In an endocentric compound, the meaning of the whole is a hyponym (a specific type) of the head. A *halfvol* glass is a type of *vol* (full) glass (specifically, one that is not empty).
*   **Linking Element:** **None (Zero Morph)**.
    *   Unlike some Dutch compounds that use an *-s-* (e.g., *liefdes*verklaring) or *-e-* (e.g., *kinder*boek), *halfvol* fuses directly.
*   **Stress Pattern:** **Initial Stress**.
    *   In Dutch compounds, primary stress typically falls on the first element.
    *   Pronunciation: **HALF**-vol (IPA: /ˈɦɑlf.vɔl/).
*   **Inflection:**
    *   Because the head (*vol*) is an adjective, the compound can be inflected when used attributively before a noun.
    *   *Example:* *een halfvol glas* (a half-full glass) vs. *een halfvolle fles* (a half-full bottle). Note the addition of the inflectional suffix *-e* on the end of the compound.

### 4. Orthography & Comparison
*   **Dutch:** Written as a single closed word (*halfvol*).
*   **English:** Often written with a hyphen (*half-full*) or as two words (*half full*), though *half-full* is the standard adjectival form.
*   **German:** Written as a single closed word (*halbvoll*).
*   **Linguistic Note:** This reflects the Germanic tendency toward compounding. The Dutch spelling convention generally prefers closed compounds for common adjectival combinations unless clarity is required.

### 5. Semantic Composition
The meaning is **compositional**, meaning the meaning of the whole is derived directly from the sum of its parts.
*   *Half* (degree) + *Vol* (state) = "In a state of being half full."

### 6. Example Usage
> "De fles is nog **halfvol**."
> *(The bottle is still half-full.)*

*   **Syntactic Role:** Predicative adjective.
*   **Morphological Note:** Here it is uninflected because it follows the verb *is*. If it were before the noun ("een halfvolle fles"), it would take the inflectional *-e*.
