From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`lijken`** is a fascinating case of **homonymy**. It represents two distinct lexical items (a verb and a noun) that share the exact same spelling and pronunciation but have different grammatical functions and slightly different semantic histories.

To analyze this correctly, we must treat them as two separate entries.

---

### 1. The Verb: *lijken* ("to seem" / "to appear")

In this context, the word functions as the **infinitive form of a verb**.

*   **Morphological Breakdown:**
    *   **Root (Stem):** `lijk-`
    *   **Suffix:** `-en`

*   **Explanation of Morphemes:**
    *   **`lijk-` (Root):** This is the lexical core. In this verbal sense, it carries the meaning of "appearance," "likeness," or "semblance." It is not referring to a physical body here, but rather the *form* something takes.
    *   **`-en` (Suffix):** This is the standard **infinitive marker** for Dutch verbs (e.g., *lopen*, *werken*, *zien*). It indicates the base form of the verb, unconjugated.

*   **Grammatical Properties:**
    *   **Verb Class:** It is a **strong verb** (Class 2).
        *   Present: *lijk* (e.g., *Het lijkt waar* - It seems true)
        *   Past: *leek* (e.g., *Het leek waar* - It seemed true)
        *   Past Participle: *geleken*
    *   **Usage:** It functions as a copula (linking verb) similar to "to be" or "to look."

*   **Etymology:**
    *   Derived from Middle Dutch *lijken*, Old Dutch *līkōn*.
    *   Cognate with the English word **"like"** (in the sense of "to be similar to," though the preposition "like" is more common now) and German *gleichen* (to resemble).
    *   Historically, the semantic shift moved from "having the form of" $\rightarrow$ "resembling" $\rightarrow$ "seeming."

---

### 2. The Noun: *lijken* ("corpses")

In this context, the word functions as the **plural form of a neuter noun**.

*   **Morphological Breakdown:**
    *   **Root (Stem):** `lijk-`
    *   **Suffix:** `-en`

*   **Explanation of Morphemes:**
    *   **`lijk-` (Root):** This is the lexical core. Here, it refers specifically to a "dead body" or "corpse." The singular form is *het lijk*.
    *   **`-en` (Suffix):** This is a common **plural marker** in Dutch (similar to the English *-s* or *-en* as in *oxen*). It indicates that there is more than one body.

*   **Grammatical Properties:**
    *   **Gender:** Neuter (*het-woord*).
    *   **Number:** Plural.
    *   **Usage:** *De lijken werden gevonden* (The corpses were found).

*   **Etymology:**
    *   Derived from Proto-Germanic **\*līką** ("body, form").
    *   Cognate with the archaic English word **"lich"** (as seen in *lichgate* or *lichfield*), German *Leiche* (corpse), and Swedish *lik*.
    *   The meaning narrowed from general "body/form" to specifically "dead body."

---

### 3. Comparative Linguistic Summary

While the surface forms are identical, the internal structure reveals a shared ancestry with divergent paths.

| Feature | Verb (*to seem*) | Noun (*corpses*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Root** | `lijk-` (Appearance/Form) | `lijk-` (Dead Body) |
| **Suffix** | `-en` (Infinitive Marker) | `-en` (Plural Marker) |
| **Word Class** | Verb (Strong) | Noun (Neuter) |
| **Singular** | *lijk* (stem) | *lijk* (het lijk) |
| **Cognate** | English *like* (verb) | English *lich* (noun) |

### 4. The Common Root: Proto-Germanic *\*līką*

Both meanings stem from the same Proto-Germanic root **\*līką**, which meant **"body"** or **"form."**

*   **Path A (The Noun):** The meaning narrowed to "physical body," and eventually, in a specific cultural context, to "dead body" (corpse).
*   **Path B (The Verb):** The meaning shifted from "having a body/form" to "having the appearance of," eventually becoming the verb "to seem."

### Conclusion
Linguistically, **`lijken`** is a **zero-derived homonym**.
1.  As a **verb**, it is a **Root + Infinitive Suffix** construction meaning "to appear."
2.  As a **noun**, it is a **Root + Plural Suffix** construction meaning "corpses."

The ambiguity is resolved entirely by **syntax** (sentence structure). If it follows a subject and precedes an adjective, it is the verb (*Het lijkt goed*). If it follows a determiner like *de* or *deze*, it is the noun (*De lijken*).
