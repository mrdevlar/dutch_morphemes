From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`ordenen`** (meaning "to order," "to arrange," or "to organize") can be broken down into three distinct morphemes.

Here is the morphological analysis:

### 1. Morpheme Breakdown

| Morpheme | Type | Function | Meaning/Origin |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ord-`** | **Root** | Semantic Core | "Order," "rank," "sequence" |
| **`-en-`** | **Derivational** | Verbalizer | Turns the root into a verb stem |
| **`-en`** | **Inflectional** | Infinitive Marker | Marks the dictionary/infinitive form |

**Full Structure:** `ord` + `en` + `en`

---

### 2. Detailed Explanation

#### A. The Root: `ord-`
*   **Status:** This is a **bound root** in modern Dutch. You cannot use `ord` as a standalone word in contemporary Dutch (unlike in English, where "order" can be a noun or verb).
*   **Etymology:** It originates from the Latin noun ***ordo*** (meaning "row," "rank," or "order").
*   **Related Word:** This root survives as the independent noun **`orde`** (the order). In the noun *orde*, the `-e` is a nominal ending, whereas in the verb *ordenen*, the root is extended differently.

#### B. The Derivational Suffix: `-en-`
*   **Function:** This is a **verbalizing suffix**. It attaches to the root `ord-` to create the verb stem **`orden-`**.
*   **Process:** This is an example of **denominal derivation** (creating a verb from a noun concept).
*   **Phonology:** The insertion of the `-n-` is etymologically significant. It comes from the Latin verb ***ordinare*** (to put in order). While the Dutch noun *orde* dropped the `n`, the verb retained it (via Middle Dutch *ordenen*).
*   **Conjugation Proof:** We know `-en-` is part of the stem because when you conjugate the verb, the `n` remains.
    *   *Ik orden* (I order)
    *   *Jij ordent* (You order)
    *   *Wij ordenen* (We order)
    *   If the stem were just `ord-`, the conjugation would look like *ik ord*, which is incorrect.

#### C. The Inflectional Suffix: `-en`
*   **Function:** This is the standard **infinitive marker** for Dutch verbs.
*   **Grammar:** In Dutch, the citation form (the form you find in a dictionary) is the infinitive. Almost all Dutch verbs end in `-en` in this form (e.g., *lopen*, *werken*, *zien* $\rightarrow$ *zien* is irregular, but *maken* $\rightarrow$ *maken*).
*   **The "Double -en":** The word *ordenen* is notable for having two `-en` sequences.
    1.  The first `-en` is part of the **stem** (derived from the root).
    2.  The second `-en` is the **infinitive ending**.
    *   *Stem:* `orden-`
    *   *Infinitive:* `orden` + `en` = `ordenen`

---

### 3. Linguistic Comparison (Dutch vs. English)

It is helpful to compare this to the English cognate **"to order"**:

*   **English:** Uses **Zero Derivation** (Conversion). The word "order" is both the noun and the verb. No morphological change occurs.
    *   Noun: *order*
    *   Verb: *order*
*   **Dutch:** Uses **Suffixation**. The noun and verb are morphologically distinct.
    *   Noun: *orde* (Root `ord-` + Nominal `-e`)
    *   Verb: *ordenen* (Root `ord-` + Verbal `-en-` + Infinitive `-en`)

### 4. Summary
The word **`ordenen`** is a regular weak verb in Dutch. It is formed by taking the Latin root for "order" (`ord-`), adding a verbalizing suffix to create the stem (`-en-`), and finally adding the standard infinitive ending (`-en`). This structure preserves the historical `n` from the Latin *ordinare*, distinguishing the verb from the noun *orde*.
