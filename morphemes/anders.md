From a linguistic perspective, the Dutch word **`anders`** is a fascinating example of how historical grammar fossilizes into modern vocabulary.

While modern speakers often perceive it as a single, unbreakable unit, a morphological and etymological analysis reveals it consists of two distinct morphemes.

Here is the breakdown:

### 1. Morphemic Breakdown

| Morpheme | Form | Type | Meaning/Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Root** | `ander-` | Lexical Stem | "Other," "different" |
| **Suffix** | `-s` | Derivational | Adverbializer (fossilized genitive) |

---

### 2. Detailed Analysis

#### A. The Root: `ander-`
*   **Category:** Adjective / Pronoun stem.
*   **Meaning:** It carries the core semantic weight of "other" or "different."
*   **Etymology:** This stems from the Proto-Germanic **\*andraz**. It is cognate with the English word **other** and the German word **ander**.
*   **Modern Usage:** In modern Dutch, this root appears in the inflected adjective *ander* (e.g., *een ander boek* / "a different book") and the pronoun *de ander* ("the other one").

#### B. The Suffix: `-s`
*   **Category:** Derivational suffix (Adverbializer).
*   **Function:** It converts the adjective *ander* into an adverb.
*   **Historical Origin (Crucial):** This is not a plural marker or a possessive marker. It is a **fossilized genitive ending**.
    *   In Old Dutch and Old High German, the neuter singular genitive form of adjectives was frequently used to create adverbs.
    *   The form was essentially *anderes* (genitive neuter of *ander*).
    *   Over time, the vowel *e* was dropped, leaving the *-s*.
*   **Linguistic Parallel:** This is the same process found in English words like **always** (from *all* + *ways* [genitive]), **besides**, **once**, and **twice**. In German, the equivalent is *anders*.

---

### 3. Synchronic vs. Diachronic Perspective

To fully understand the word linguistically, one must distinguish between how it is analyzed historically versus how it functions today.

*   **Diachronic (Historical) Analysis:**
    As explained above, it is clearly **`ander` + `-s`**. The *-s* was once a living grammatical case ending indicating the genitive neuter used adverbially.

*   **Synchronic (Modern) Analysis:**
    In contemporary Dutch, the *-s* is no longer a productive suffix. You cannot take a random adjective and add *-s* to make it an adverb (e.g., you cannot say *`snel`* to mean "quickly" in the same way; you use *snel* or *snelweg*).
    Therefore, in modern morphology, **`anders`** is often treated as a **monomorphemic lexicalized adverb**. The connection to *ander* is still semantically transparent, but the *-s* is no longer analyzed by speakers as a separate grammatical unit.

### 4. Syntactic Function

*   **Adverb:** It modifies verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.
    *   *Hij denkt **anders**.* (He thinks **differently**.)
    *   *Het is **anders**.* (It is **different** / **otherwise**.)
*   **Predicative Adjective:** In Dutch, *anders* can function as a predicative adjective (following a copula), whereas in English, we would strictly use the adverb "differently" or the adjective "different."
    *   *De situatie is **anders**.* (The situation is **different**.)

### 5. Summary

From a linguistic perspective, **`anders`** is a **derivational adverb** formed from the root **`ander-`** ("other") and a fossilized **genitive suffix `-s`**. While it functions as a single lexical unit in modern Dutch, its structure preserves a relic of the Germanic case system where the genitive neuter was used to denote manner or state.
